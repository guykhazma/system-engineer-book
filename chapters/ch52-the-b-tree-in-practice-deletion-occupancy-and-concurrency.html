Chapter 51 left the B-tree half-built. It could grow — insert descends to a leaf and, if the leaf is full, splits, pushing a separator key up and growing the tree only at the root — and it could search in a handful of page fetches. But a table is not append-only: rows are deleted, and an index that only ever grows is a leak. A B-tree is also not built once and frozen: it is written by many transactions at once, and a structure that must be locked whole for every insert serves one writer at a time. And the split that keeps the tree balanced has a quiet cost Chapter 51 flagged but did not measure — it leaves pages half-empty, so a B-tree built by ordinary inserts is markedly larger than the data it indexes. This chapter closes those three gaps, which is the difference between the textbook B-tree and the one PostgreSQL or InnoDB actually ships: deletion with its borrow-or-merge rebalancing, the mirror image of the split, that shrinks the tree at the root (§52.1); why real systems usually defer that merge and what index bloat that buys and costs (§52.2); occupancy — the measured fact that random insertion settles nodes at about 69% full, and why bulk-loading reaches nearly 100% (§52.3); and concurrency — latch coupling and the Blink-tree right-link that let many threads descend the same tree without locking it whole, with its named trap, the split race (§52.4); then what all of this carries into the write-optimized engine that comes next (§52.5).

52.1 Deletion: underflow, borrow, and merge

Deletion begins exactly as search and insert do — descend to the leaf where the key lives — and then removes the entry, which is trivial unless it drops the node below the balance invariant's floor. Chapter 51's invariant says every node except the root holds at least ⌈m/2⌉−1 keys (about half a full node); a leaf that falls below that has underflowed, and the tree must be repaired before the deletion is done, because an under-full node breaks the guarantee that gives search its uniform cost. There are two repairs, tried in order. First, borrow (also called redistribution): if an adjacent sibling has more than the minimum, move one of its keys across — rotating it through the parent, since the separator key in the parent must always sit between the two children — and the underflow is fixed with a single key movement and no structural change. Second, if no sibling can spare a key (both are exactly at the minimum), merge: combine the under-full node with a sibling into one node, and pull the separator key down from the parent to sit between them, since with the two children fused that separator no longer separates anything. Merging is the exact mirror of a split — a split creates a node and pushes a key up; a merge destroys a node and pulls a key down — and it has the same propagation: pulling the separator down removes a key from the parent, which may itself underflow and merge, and so on up the tree. If the merge propagates all the way to the root and the root is left with no keys and a single child, the root is removed and that single child becomes the new root — and this is the only way a B-tree's height ever decreases. So the tree grows at the root by splitting and shrinks at the root by merging; it is symmetric, and that symmetry is why all leaves stay at the same depth through any mix of inserts and deletes. Search never sees an unbalanced tree because deletion, like insertion, does the balancing work up front.

52.2 Lazy deletion and index bloat: why systems defer the merge

The borrow-and-merge of §52.1 is the correct deletion, and it is what a textbook implements. Most production B-trees do not do it on every delete. The reason is cost and concurrency: a merge touches three pages at once — the under-full node, its sibling, and the parent — and must lock all three against other operations, and the merge can propagate upward and lock still more; under a heavy concurrent workload that is a lot of contention to spend on the common case where a deleted key's slot will soon be filled by another insert. So real engines mostly use lazy deletion: remove the entry (or just mark it dead), let the node sit under-full, and reclaim a page to the storage manager's free list (Chapter 50) only when it becomes completely empty, rebalancing rarely or never. This is a deliberate trade of space for speed and simplicity, and it has a name for its cost: index bloat. Because under-full pages are tolerated rather than merged, a B-tree that has seen many deletes keeps its page count even as its live-key count falls — the on-disk index stays large while the data it indexes shrinks, occupancy drops toward the deleted fraction, and range scans still walk the mostly-empty pages, paying page fetches for keys that are gone. The bloat is not a bug; it is the chosen point on the space/latency curve. It is undone deliberately, not automatically: a rebuild (PostgreSQL's REINDEX, or dropping and recreating the index) reconstructs the tree densely, and background reclamation (PostgreSQL's VACUUM, InnoDB's purge) returns wholly-empty pages. Knowing that deletion is usually lazy — that the index does not shrink itself — is the difference between “the delete is broken” and “the index needs a rebuild,” and it is exactly the confusion the communicate box below untangles.

(Answer to the §52.1 quick check: the two repairs are borrow from a sibling that has a spare key, tried first, and merge with a sibling when neither can spare one. Only merge can change the height, and only when it propagates to the root and empties it: the root is removed and its single child becomes the new root, so — like growth — the height change happens at the root.)

52.3 Node occupancy: random inserts settle at ln 2, bulk-loading fills the pages

How full is a B-tree's nodes, on average? The answer decides how big the index is for a given amount of data, and it is not 100%. Chapter 51's naive split cuts a full node in half, so a just-split node is 50% full; subsequent inserts refill it, and averaged over a long random insert sequence the occupancy settles at a specific constant. Yao proved in 1978 that for B-trees of reasonable order, random insertion drives the average node occupancy to ln 2 ≈ 0.693 — about 69% full — and the working B-tree here reproduces it. Building trees by inserting 200,000 keys in random order and measuring keys-held over keys-that-fit:

random-insertion B-tree, average node occupancy (Yao 1978: -> ln2 = 0.6931):
  min-degree t= 16 (<= 31 keys/node), N=200000: occupancy=0.687, height=4
  min-degree t= 32 (<= 63 keys/node), N=200000: occupancy=0.686, height=4
  min-degree t= 64 (<=127 keys/node), N=200000: occupancy=0.684, height=3

Every measurement lands within a hair of ln 2. The consequence is quantitative and large: a B-tree built by ordinary row-by-row insertion is about 1/ln 2 ≈ 1.44× the size of the same keys packed densely — it needs roughly 44% more pages than the data strictly requires, which is more pages in the buffer pool, more page fetches for a scan, and (because lower occupancy means lower effective fanout) the occasional extra level of height. This is why databases distinguish bulk-loading from insertion: given data already sorted, an engine builds the B-tree bottom-up — fill a leaf completely, emit it, start the next; when a level's worth of leaves exists, build their parent — which packs every node to nearly 100% and produces the smallest, shallowest possible tree. It is both faster (no per-key descent, no splits) and denser than inserting the same rows one at a time, which is why “CREATE INDEX on an existing table” and “a rebuild” use bulk-load, and why append-heavy inserts use the append-aware split of Chapter 51 to approach the same density. The occupancy constant is also the reason a healthy index is still noticeably larger than its raw data: 69%, not 100%, is normal for a live, randomly-updated tree.

52.4 Concurrent access: latch coupling and the Blink-tree

Everything so far assumed one thread. A real B-tree is descended and modified by many transactions at once, and the pages it touches live in the shared buffer pool (Chapter 48), so access must be protected by latches — the short-held mutexes on in-memory pages, distinct from the transactional locks of a later part. The naive protection, one latch over the whole tree, allows exactly one operation at a time and wastes the machine. The classic improvement is latch coupling, also called crabbing: as a search descends, it latches a child before releasing the parent — always holding the ground under its feet, like a crab moving one leg at a time — so a concurrent modification can never move a node out from under it mid-descent. For a read that is enough and cheap: take a shared latch on the child, release the parent, move down, so only one or two latches are held at any moment. An insert is harder, because it might split, and a split modifies the parent — so a crabbing insert must keep every ancestor latched until it reaches a node that is provably safe (has room, so it cannot split and cannot push a change upward); only then can it release the ancestors above. When nodes are near-full, “safe” is rare, ancestor latches are held long, and concurrency collapses back toward serial. The elegant fix is the Blink-tree of Lehman and Yao (1981): give every node a right-link pointer to its right sibling and record each node's high key (the largest key it covers). A split now adds the new node to the right and links to it, and it does so as a purely local, left-to-right operation. A reader that arrives at a node just after it split, and finds its target key is now greater than the node's high key, simply follows the right-link to the new sibling instead of needing the parent to have been updated first. Because a searcher can always recover a mid-split move by moving right, it never has to hold a parent latch to guard against a split below it — a descent holds only one latch at a time. That single structural addition, the right-link, is what let B-trees scale to many concurrent threads, and it is why the Blink-tree is the concurrency design real systems (including PostgreSQL) build on.

Trap: naive latch coupling either serializes the tree or deadlocks — the split race

The tempting concurrency scheme is “just latch-couple everything with exclusive latches, parent held until the child is latched, all the way down” — and it fails two ways at once. First, it serializes writers: to be safe against a split propagating up, a naive insert must hold exclusive latches on the whole root-to-leaf path until it finds a node with room, so under a write-heavy load every insert latches the root exclusively and writers run one at a time — the tree's own root becomes the bottleneck. The over-correction is to release ancestor latches early to regain concurrency, which triggers the second failure, the split race: a searcher releases a parent latch, a concurrent insert splits the child and moves half its keys (including the searcher's target) to a new right sibling, and the searcher — now latching a node that no longer holds its key — reports the key absent though it is present. Worse, trying to fix this by re-latching upward invites deadlock: descents take latches top-down while a propagating split takes them bottom-up, so two operations can grab the same two nodes in opposite orders and wait on each other forever. “Hold exclusive latches down the whole path” and “release early for concurrency” are not both safe under naive coupling — one serializes, the other loses keys or deadlocks. The honest fix is not a latching tweak but the structural one: the Blink-tree right-link and high key, which make a split locally recoverable so a searcher moves right instead of holding an ancestor latch — one latch at a time, no path-long exclusive holds, no opposite-order deadlock.

Say it like a systems engineer

Try it first. A teammate says: “We archived and deleted about 90% of the rows in our biggest table last week. But the index on that table is barely smaller on disk than before, and the range queries didn't get faster. The delete must be broken — should I file a bug?” Cover the paragraph and answer in four or five sentences, drawing on §52.2 and §52.3.

Model answer. “It's almost certainly not a bug — it's how B-tree deletion normally works. Most engines delete lazily: they mark the entries dead and leave the pages under-full rather than merging siblings on every delete, because eager merges are expensive under concurrency. So the index keeps its page count even though 90% of the keys are gone — that's index bloat, and it's why the on-disk size barely moved and scans still walk the now-mostly-empty pages. The index doesn't shrink itself; you reclaim the space deliberately, by rebuilding it — REINDEX or drop-and-recreate, which bulk-loads it back to near-100% fill — and by letting VACUUM/purge reclaim the empty pages. One more expectation to set: even a freshly rebuilt, healthy index is only about 69% full on average under random inserts, so it will always be somewhat larger than the raw data — don't expect the index size to equal the live-row size.” Naming lazy deletion, index bloat, the rebuild as the fix, and the ln 2 occupancy baseline is the senior register.

(Answer to the §52.3 quick check: random insertion settles nodes at about 69% occupancy — the constant ln 2 ≈ 0.693, as Yao proved and the code measured (0.684–0.687). An index built by insertion therefore carries about 1/ln 2 ≈ 1.44× — ~44% more — pages than the same keys bulk-loaded. Bulk-loading packs tighter because it builds bottom-up from sorted data, filling each node completely and never splitting, so no node is left half-empty.)

52.5 What to carry forward

Deletion is the mirror of the split: descend, remove, and if the node underflows, first borrow a key from a sibling through the parent, or if none can spare one, merge with a sibling and pull the separator down — a merge can propagate up and, if it empties the root, remove it, which is the only way the tree's height shrinks, at the root, symmetric with how it grows (§52.1). Real systems mostly defer that merge — lazy deletion — tolerating under-full pages and reclaiming only empty ones, trading space for concurrency and paying in index bloat that a rebuild (REINDEX) undoes (§52.2). Occupancy under random insertion settles at ln 2 ≈ 69% (Yao 1978, reproduced at 0.684–0.687), so an inserted index is ~1.44× the pages of a bulk-loaded one, which is why databases bulk-load sorted data bottom-up to pack near 100% (§52.3). Concurrency uses latch coupling (crabbing) to descend without locking the tree whole, and the Blink-tree right-link and high key (Lehman & Yao 1981) to make a split locally recoverable so a searcher holds one latch at a time — the structural fix for the split-race trap that naive coupling falls into (§52.4).

With Chapters 51 and 52 the B-tree is complete: a wide, shallow, self-balancing tree of pages that gives selective look-up, range scan, ordered iteration, safe deletion, and concurrent access — the workhorse index of every relational database. But notice the shape of everything this chapter added. Deletion rebalances pages in place. Keeping occupancy up means splitting and packing pages in place. Concurrency exists precisely because writers are modifying pages in place and must be kept from colliding. The entire chapter is the machinery of updating data in place — rewriting the page where a key belongs — done safely and densely. That in-place discipline is exactly what makes the B-tree superb for reads and range scans and exactly what makes every write a random page update, paying the Chapter 46 random-write cost, and what makes concurrent writers contend on the same hot pages. The next chapter takes the opposite bet. Instead of finding the right page and updating it in place, it never updates in place: it buffers writes in memory and flushes them as new, immutable, sorted files, turning every random write into a sequential one — and pays the bill later, at read time, by having to merge those files. That is the log-structured merge-tree, the write-optimized counterpart to the read-optimized B-tree, and the whole rest of this part is the two families and when each wins. The B-tree updates in place; the LSM-tree appends and merges; and the tension between them is the central storage-engine decision.
