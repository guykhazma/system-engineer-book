Chapter 53 built the LSM-tree's write and read paths and ended at an uncomfortable place: the write path only ever adds immutable SSTables, so their number grows without bound, dead versions of updated keys and tombstones of deleted ones accumulate, and the read path pays for every file — read amplification and space that climb forever if nothing intervenes. Compaction is what intervenes. It is a background process that takes several SSTables, merges their sorted contents into fewer new SSTables, and along the way keeps only the newest version of each key and drops tombstones once it has passed the values they shadow — bounding the file count (and thus read amplification) and reclaiming the space of dead data. But compaction is not free: it re-reads and re-writes data that was already written, which is write amplification, the LSM-tree's own cost of keeping order, deferred and sequential where the B-tree's was immediate and random. So an LSM-tree is defined by a three-way tension — how much extra reading, extra writing, and extra space it accepts — and the compaction strategy is how you pick your point in that space. This chapter makes the trade precise: what compaction actually does, merging runs and dropping dead data, reproduced on real runs (§54.1); the three amplifications — write, read, space — and the impossibility, the RUM tension, that you cannot minimize all three (§54.2); the two great compaction families, leveled and size-tiered, and an arithmetic model of where each sits on the three axes (§54.3); the tuning that follows and its named trap, that compaction is not free background work but a source of write stalls and device wear (§54.4); and what this completes about the two storage-engine families (§54.5).

54.1 Compaction: merging runs, dropping dead versions and tombstones

Compaction is a k-way merge of sorted runs, and it is cheap for the same reason the flush was: the inputs are already sorted, so merging them is one linear pass (the merge step of mergesort), reading sequentially and writing sequentially — the ~916 MB/s side of Chapter 46, never a random access. As it merges, compaction does the garbage collection the immutable write path could not. When several runs contain the same key, it keeps only the newest version and discards the rest — reclaiming the space of every superseded update. And when it meets a tombstone, it drops the tombstone and the older values it shadows, since after the merge there is no older run left for those values to hide in — reclaiming deleted data and, finally, the tombstone itself. The output is fewer, larger sorted runs holding only live data, so a subsequent read touches fewer files (lower read amplification) over less dead space (lower space amplification). The working model compacts twenty overlapping runs full of updates and tombstones:

concrete compaction of 20 SSTable runs (updates + tombstones, keyspace 5000):
  entries across runs before: 25934   (a point miss scans all 20 runs)
  entries after full compaction: 3362 (a point read scans 1 run; dead versions + tombstones dropped)
  space reclaimed: 87.0%   read amplification: 20x -> 1x

The twenty runs held 25,934 entries, but most were dead — superseded updates and tombstones — so merging them to keep only the newest live value per key left 3,362 entries in one run: 87% of the physical data was garbage that compaction reclaimed, and read amplification for the compacted range fell from twenty files to one. That is the entire value proposition of compaction in one measurement — it converts the write path's accumulated slack back into a compact, cheap-to-read form. But look at what it cost to get there: to produce the 3,362-entry output, compaction had to read all 25,934 input entries and write 3,362 out, work spent re-processing data that was already on disk. Do this repeatedly, as an LSM-tree must — every byte is rewritten each time its run is compacted into a larger one — and the rewriting adds up to several times the original data volume. That is write amplification, and it is not a flaw to be fixed but the price of order; the only question is how much of it you pay, which depends entirely on how you compact.

54.2 The three amplifications, and why you cannot minimize all three

An LSM-tree's behavior is captured by three amplification factors, each a ratio of physical work or space to the logical minimum. Write amplification is the bytes actually written to the device per byte of logical data — greater than one because compaction rewrites data as it merges runs into larger ones. Read amplification is the number of physical accesses per logical read — greater than one because a lookup probes multiple runs. Space amplification is the bytes on disk per byte of live data — greater than one because dead versions and tombstones linger until compaction removes them. Here is the fact that makes LSM tuning an art rather than a lookup: these three are in tension, and lowering one tends to raise another. Compact aggressively — merge runs the moment they appear — and you keep few runs (low read amplification) and little dead data (low space amplification), but you rewrite constantly (high write amplification). Compact lazily — let runs pile up before merging — and you rewrite rarely (low write amplification) but read across many runs (high read amplification) over lots of dead data (high space amplification). You cannot have all three small at once. This is a specific instance of a general law of access-method design, the RUM conjecture of Athanassoulis and colleagues (2016): optimizing any two of read overhead, update overhead, and memory/space overhead comes at the expense of the third. The LSM-tree does not escape the trade the B-tree faced; it relocates it — the B-tree spent its cost as immediate random writes and tight space, the LSM-tree spends its as deferred sequential write amplification and looser space — but the trilemma is the same, and answering the opener's second question: no single compaction strategy makes all three amplifications small. You choose which to spend.

(Answer to the §54.1 quick check: compaction reclaims superseded update versions (keeping only the newest value per key) and deleted data — when it meets a tombstone it drops both the tombstone and the older values it shadowed, since no older run remains to hide them. The merge is cheap because the inputs are sorted, so it is one sequential pass (Chapter 46's fast side), never a random access. The new cost is write amplification: repeatedly rewriting data as it is merged into larger runs writes several times the logical data volume over an item's lifetime.)

54.3 Leveled versus size-tiered compaction: the model

The two dominant compaction strategies sit at opposite ends of the write-versus-read-and-space trade, and both organize SSTables into levels, each level holding runs some size ratio T larger than the one above; the number of levels is L ≈ log-base-T of the data size over the memtable size, so L is small (single digits) for the same logarithmic reason the B-tree is shallow. Leveled compaction keeps exactly one sorted run per level: when a level overflows, its data is merged into the next level's single run, which — because that level is T times bigger — rewrites roughly T bytes for each byte promoted, so write amplification is about T×L; but a read finds at most one run per level (low read amplification) and dead data is squeezed out quickly (low space amplification, near 1). Size-tiered compaction instead lets up to T runs accumulate per level and merges them, all at once, only when a level is full: each byte is written just once per level, so write amplification is only about L; but a read may probe up to T runs at every level (high read amplification, ~T×L) and a full level's worth of dead data can linger (space amplification up to ~T). Same structure, two policies, opposite corners. The model computes all three amplifications for a billion-entry store as the size ratio T varies:

amplification model (N=1e9 entries, flush unit B=1e6; W=write, R=read, S=space amp):
  T    levels | LEVELED  (W / R / S)     | SIZE-TIERED (W / R / S)
   2     10   |   20 /  10 / 1.50        |    10 /  20 /  2.0
   4      5   |   20 /   5 / 1.25        |     5 /  20 /  4.0
  10      3   |   30 /   3 / 1.10        |     3 /  30 / 10.0
  20      3   |   60 /   3 / 1.05        |     3 /  60 / 20.0
  -> leveled trades WRITE amp for low READ and SPACE amp; size-tiered does the reverse. Same tree.

Read the table across and the trade is stark and symmetric. At every size ratio, leveled compaction's read and space amplification are low (a handful of runs, space within ~10–50% of the live data) while it pays in write amplification (20–60×); size-tiered's write amplification is low (3–10×) while it pays in read amplification (up to 60×) and space (up to 2–20× the live data). Raising T makes the tree shallower — fewer levels — which cuts one column and inflates the other: leveled's write amplification climbs with T while its read and space fall; size-tiered's read and space climb with T while its write falls. There is no row where all three are small, which is the RUM tension made numeric. This is why real engines expose compaction as a choice: RocksDB defaults to leveled for read-heavy workloads that need tight space, offers size-tiered (universal) compaction for write-heavy ingest that can tolerate read and space blowup, and Cassandra, HBase, and ScyllaDB expose the same knob — because the “right” answer is a property of the workload, not of the structure.

The same idea three ways: leveled and size-tiered are opposite corners of the write/read/space trade

1. In words. Leveled keeps one run per level, so reads and space are tight but every promotion rewrites a full larger level — high write amplification. Size-tiered lets runs pile up and merges in bulk, so each byte is written about once per level — low write amplification — but reads probe many runs over much dead data. You pick the corner your workload can afford.

2. As a picture. Figure 54.1: a triangle with corners labeled read, write, and space; a “leveled” dot pulled toward low-read/low-space (far from write), a “size-tiered” dot pulled toward low-write (far from read and space) — no dot can sit at the center.

3. As numbers. Modeled here at size ratio T=10 over a billion entries: leveled = write 30 / read 3 / space 1.10; size-tiered = write 3 / read 30 / space 10. The write and read columns literally swap, and space swings 1.1× vs 10× — the trilemma, quantified.

Trap: compaction is not free background work — write stalls, write amplification, and device wear

“Compaction runs in the background, so it's free” is the trap, and it fails at three levels. First, throughput: compaction competes with foreground writes and reads for the same device bandwidth and buffer pool (Chapter 48), so a store under heavy ingest can find its reads and writes slowed by the very merging meant to help them — the background work is real work on the same disk. Second, and sharper, write stalls: if the ingest rate exceeds the rate at which compaction can merge, the unmerged runs (RocksDB's level 0, for instance) pile up, read amplification spikes, and the engine must throttle or stall incoming writes to let compaction catch up — the LSM-tree's “fast writes” turn into periodic write stalls exactly when the write load is highest, which is a classic production surprise. Third, device wear: write amplification means the SSD physically writes several times the logical data — a leveled store at write amplification 30 writes thirty bytes per logical byte — which multiplies flash wear and can dominate the storage budget. The two counter-traps are equal and opposite: switching to size-tiered to cut write amplification (and stalls and wear) can multiply read amplification and double or 10× the space; switching to leveled for read speed and tight space can triple or sextuple the write amplification and its stalls and wear. “Compaction is free” and “just pick the cheap-write policy” are both false: compaction is foreground-affecting work you provision for, and every policy spends one amplification to save another. The honest posture is to measure the workload's read/write/space mix, choose the compaction family that spends the amplification you can most afford, and provision compaction throughput so it keeps up with ingest.

Say it like a systems engineer

Try it first. A teammate proposes: “Our LSM stores are wearing out SSDs and I've read that size-tiered compaction has much lower write amplification. Let's switch every table to size-tiered to cut the writes and save the flash.” Cover the paragraph and answer in four or five sentences, drawing on §54.2–§54.3.

Model answer. “Size-tiered really does cut write amplification — at size ratio 10 it's about 3× versus leveled's ~30×, so the flash-wear worry is legitimate for write-heavy tables. But it isn't free: size-tiered raises the other two amplifications by roughly the same factor it saves. Read amplification can jump to ~30× because a lookup probes many runs per level, and space amplification can hit ~10× because a level's worth of dead versions and tombstones lingers before the bulk merge — so our read-heavy tables would get slower and our disk usage could balloon. The right move is per-table, not global: keep leveled where reads and tight space matter, use size-tiered only on the write-heavy ingest tables that can tolerate the read and space cost, and either way provision enough compaction throughput that we don't hit write stalls under peak ingest. It's a three-way trade — read, write, space — and we should spend the amplification each table can most afford, not minimize writes everywhere.” Naming the RUM trade, the specific read/space cost of size-tiered, and the per-workload choice is the senior register.

54.4 Tuning: choosing which amplification to spend

Everything above resolves into a single design decision made per store, sometimes per table: which amplification can this workload most afford to spend? A read-heavy or space-constrained workload — an index served under tight latency, a store on expensive fast storage — wants leveled compaction, accepting high write amplification to keep reads and space tight. A write-heavy ingest workload — a metrics or event firehose, a log store — wants size-tiered, accepting read and space blowup to keep write amplification and device wear low. The size ratio T is the finer knob within each: larger T means fewer levels, which for leveled raises write amplification while lowering read and space, and for size-tiered does the reverse — so T slides you along the same trade the family already chose. And whatever the choice, compaction throughput must be provisioned to keep pace with ingest, or the write stalls of the trap appear regardless of family. There is no universally correct setting because there is no workload-independent point in a three-way trade; the competent move is to characterize the workload's read/write/space sensitivity and pick the corner accordingly, which is exactly why every production LSM engine ships the choice as configuration rather than baking one in. The tuning is not a defect of the LSM-tree; it is the LSM-tree honestly exposing the trilemma the RUM conjecture says no access method can escape.

54.5 What to carry forward

Compaction is the background k-way merge that completes the LSM-tree: it merges sorted runs in one sequential pass, keeping only the newest version of each key and dropping tombstones with the values they shadow, which bounds read amplification and reclaims space (the model reclaimed 87% dead data and cut read amplification 20×→1×) — at the cost of write amplification from rewriting data as it merges (§54.1). An LSM-tree trades among three amplifications — write (bytes written per logical byte), read (accesses per logical read), and space (bytes stored per live byte) — and the RUM tension (Athanassoulis et al. 2016) says no strategy makes all three small (§54.2). Leveled compaction (one run per level) minimizes read and space at high write amplification ~T×L; size-tiered (many runs per level, bulk-merged) minimizes write at high read ~T×L and space ~T — opposite corners, quantified by the model (§54.3). The choice is per-workload, the size ratio T is the finer knob, and compaction throughput must keep pace with ingest or write stalls and device wear appear — compaction is provisioned work, not free (§54.4).

With this chapter the two storage-engine families are complete and fully drawn. The B-tree keeps data sorted on disk and updates it in place: excellent reads and range scans, random and immediate write cost, tight ~69% space, and a single point on the trade. The LSM-tree keeps data sorted in memory and appends immutable sorted runs, using compaction to bound the accumulation: cheap sequential writes, read and space costs paid through amplification, and — through the compaction strategy — a whole curve of points on the RUM trade you can slide along. That last difference is the deep one: the B-tree offers one balance of read, write, and space, while the LSM-tree offers a tunable family of balances, which is why write-optimized engines dominate high-ingest systems that need to place their cost deliberately. Two threads remain open for the rest of the part. The read amplification this chapter bounds but does not eliminate — a point read still probes several runs — is attacked next by the Bloom filter, a tiny in-memory structure that lets a read skip runs that provably cannot contain the key, cutting the probes toward one. And the whole question the part has been building toward — given a concrete workload, B-tree or LSM-tree, and at what settings — is answered in the part's final chapter, now that both engines and the amplification vocabulary to compare them are in hand. The B-tree optimizes reads; the LSM-tree optimizes writes and hands you the dial; and knowing which cost your workload can afford to spend is the whole of storage-engine judgment.
