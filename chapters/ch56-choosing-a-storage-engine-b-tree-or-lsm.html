Chapter 56 — Choosing a Storage Engine: B-Tree or LSM, and at What Settings

Before you start: Chapter 46 (sequential ~916 MB/s versus random ~19 MB/s — the ~48× gap that makes a B-tree's in-place write expensive and the LSM's sequential write cheap, the single number that drives this chapter's model), Chapter 51 and Chapter 52 (the B-tree: one point on the trade — good reads and range scans, random in-place writes, ~69% space), Chapter 53 and Chapter 54 (the LSM-tree, compaction, and the three amplifications with the RUM tension), Chapter 55 (Bloom filters, which pull LSM point-read amplification back near the B-tree's, so the surviving differences are range scans and the write/space trade).  ·  The part has built two storage engines and a full vocabulary — read, write, and space amplification; sequential versus random; compaction families; Bloom filters — for comparing them. This capstone spends that vocabulary on the one question a database designer cannot avoid: given a concrete workload, B-tree or LSM-tree, and at what settings?

Before you read on

From memory: (1) Chapter 54 — name the three amplifications an LSM-tree trades among and state the RUM tension in one line. (2) Chapter 46 and Chapter 53 — a B-tree's writes are random and in place; an LSM-tree's are sequential appends. Which of Chapter 46's two bandwidths does each pay, and roughly what is the ratio between them? (3) Predict: you must pick one engine for each of three stores — a read-mostly key-value cache, an analytics table scanned in ranges, and a metrics firehose ingesting millions of points a second. Write your pick for each and one reason; §56.3–§56.4 score them against a model and check your intuition.

Everything since Chapter 46 has been assembling the terms of a single decision. Two engines are now fully drawn. The B-tree keeps data sorted on disk and updates it in place: excellent point reads and unbeatable range scans, but every write is a random device access at Chapter 46's slow side, and it occupies one fixed point on the read/write/space trade at about 69% space utilization. The LSM-tree keeps data sorted in memory and appends immutable sorted runs, compacting in the background: sequential writes at the fast side, point reads pulled back near the B-tree's by Bloom filters (Chapter 55), but read and space costs paid through amplification — and, through the compaction family and size ratio, a whole tunable curve of balances rather than a single point. The choice between them is not a matter of which is “better”; it is a matter of which cost a given workload can most afford to spend, which is the RUM conjecture (Chapter 54) restated as a decision. This chapter makes that decision concrete: what the question actually is and why it has no workload-independent answer (§56.1); the three engines' cost vectors — exactly what each charges on point reads, range scans, writes, and space (§56.2); a small analytical model that scores the three against named workloads, reproduced on real runs (§56.3); how to read the model's verdicts — and, just as important, where the model lies and measurement must take over (§56.4); and what this completes about storage engines as the part closes and durability begins (§56.5).

On the code in this chapter. One Python program (CPython 3.10.12 on this machine's 12th Gen Intel Core i7-12700H) that builds an analytical cost model, not a benchmark. It assigns each engine a cost vector — point-read accesses, range-scan run-touches, a write cost, and a space amplification — using the amplification arithmetic of Chapter 54 (leveled ~T×L write / ~L read / ~1 space; size-tiered the reverse), the Chapter 55 result that Bloom filters make point reads ~1 run, and one hardware-anchored constant: Chapter 46's sequential-to-random bandwidth ratio (~916/19 ≈ 48), applied to the B-tree's random writes so its in-place update is charged the random penalty the LSM avoids. It then scores three named workloads by a transparent weighted sum and reports the winner. Every number is exact model arithmetic of parameters stated in the code — hardware-independent except for the one cited Chapter 46 ratio — and is labeled a model, not a measurement; no wall-clock timing is taken (perf is not installed here), because the chapter's claim is about relative cost structure, which the model captures and a single machine's benchmark would only obscure.

56.1 The decision, and why it has no workload-free answer

The question “B-tree or LSM-tree?” feels like it should have a best answer, and it does not — for the same reason the RUM conjecture (Chapter 54) says no access method minimizes read, update, and space at once. An engine is a point or a curve in that three-dimensional cost space, and “best” is only defined once you say how much you weight each axis, which is a property of the workload, not of the engine. A read-mostly store that almost never writes barely feels the B-tree's random-write cost and enjoys its cheap reads and tight space; a write-saturated firehose feels that same random-write cost as its dominant expense and is desperate for the LSM's sequential appends, whatever it must pay back in read and space amplification. Neither engine is wrong; they are tuned for different points, and the workload decides which point it is standing on. This is why the honest form of the question is not “which engine is better” but “which amplification can this workload most afford to spend, and which engine (at which settings) spends that one?” — and why every serious storage system ships the choice as configuration. To answer it for a concrete workload you need two things: the cost vector of each engine (what it charges on each kind of operation) and the weights of the workload (how much of each kind it does, and how much it values space). Multiply and sum, and the “best” engine is simply the one with the lowest weighted cost — for that workload. The next two sections build exactly that.

56.2 The three cost vectors: what each engine charges

Reduce each engine to what it costs on four things: a point read, a range scan, a write, and the space it occupies — the cost vector of §56.1. The numbers come straight from the part. Point read: a B-tree fetches ~1 leaf; a leveled LSM with Bloom filters (Chapter 55) touches ~1 run; a size-tiered LSM with filters also resolves a hit in ~1 run — so all three are about 1 on point reads, which is the Bloom filter's gift. Range scan: a B-tree scans one clustered, sorted run; a leveled LSM must merge ~L runs (one per level); a size-tiered LSM must merge up to ~T×L runs — and the filter cannot help, because order is what a scan needs and hashing destroys it (Chapter 55). Write: here the sequential-versus-random distinction of Chapter 46 is decisive. A B-tree's write amplification is ~1 but random, so it pays the slow ~19 MB/s side; a leveled LSM's write amplification is ~T×L but sequential, at ~916 MB/s; a size-tiered LSM's is only ~L and sequential. To compare them we charge the B-tree's random write a penalty equal to Chapter 46's bandwidth ratio (~48×), which is why a “write amplification of 1” is not cheap when it is random. Space: a B-tree sits near 1.45× live data (~69% fill); leveled ~1.10; size-tiered ~T (here 10). The model prints the vector:

per-engine cost vector (T=10, L=5, rho=48.2):
  engine            point-rd   range(runs)   write-cost(=WA*rho?)   space-amp
  B-tree              1.00          1.0              48.2             1.45
  LSM leveled         1.00          5.0              50.0             1.10
  LSM size-tiered     1.00         50.0               5.0            10.00

Read the vector across and the two families' personalities are exact. All three are equal on point reads — the Bloom filter erased that difference. The B-tree owns the range scan (1 versus 5 or 50) because its data is clustered and sorted in one place. The B-tree's write cost (48) is high not because it writes much but because it writes randomly; leveled LSM's is about the same (50) because it rewrites a lot even though sequentially; size-tiered LSM's is the lowest by far (5) because it writes little and sequentially. And space splits the LSM family in two: leveled is tightest of all (1.10), size-tiered the loosest (10). No engine wins every column — that is the RUM tension made into a table — so the winner depends entirely on which columns the workload weights, which the model computes next.

The same idea three ways: the engine choice is a weighted sum, not a verdict

1. In words. Each engine has a cost vector (point read, range scan, write, space); each workload has weights (how much of each it does). The best engine is the one with the lowest weighted sum for that workload — so the answer is a function of the workload, not a fixed ranking of engines.

2. As a picture. Figure 56.1: three bars per workload (B-tree, leveled, size-tiered), heights the weighted cost; the shortest bar changes from workload to workload — B-tree shortest under range-heavy, size-tiered shortest under write-heavy, B-tree and leveled tied under read-heavy.

3. As numbers. At the weights modeled here: read-heavy → B-tree 8.5, leveled 8.7 (a near tie, the Bloom filter's doing); range-heavy → B-tree 6.2 beats leveled 9.2 and size-tiered 49.6; write-heavy firehose → size-tiered 14.2 beats B-tree 38.9 and leveled 40.8. Same three engines, three different winners.

56.3 The model: scoring engines against workloads

Give each workload three operation weights (point read, range scan, write) and a space sensitivity, multiply by the cost vectors of §56.2, and the lowest weighted sum names the engine to pick. The model runs three named workloads: a read-heavy point-lookup store (80% point reads, 15% writes, 5% ranges), a range-scan-heavy analytics table (80% ranges), and a write-heavy metrics firehose (80% writes, and — because it lives on cheap disk — a low space sensitivity of 0.3):

weighted cost per op (lower=better); space penalty=(space_amp-1)*space_weight:
  workload                          B-tree   leveled   size-tiered   winner
  read-heavy point lookups            8.5      8.7        13.1      B-tree
  analytics range-scan heavy          6.2      9.2        49.6      B-tree
  metrics/event ingest firehose      38.9     40.8        14.2      LSM size-tiered

Three workloads, three verdicts, and each is the RUM tension resolved by the weights. The read-heavy store is nearly a tie (B-tree 8.5, leveled 8.7): with Bloom filters the leveled LSM's point reads match the B-tree's, and its tight ~1.10 space is close too, so the B-tree wins only by a hair on the small write and range fractions — a real result, and a reminder that a read-optimized LSM is a perfectly respectable choice for a read-heavy store, not a mistake. The range-scan analytics table is a clear B-tree win (6.2 versus 9.2 and 49.6): clustered sorted storage scans one run where the LSMs merge many, and no Bloom filter closes that gap. The write firehose flips hard to size-tiered (14.2 versus 38.9 and 40.8): 80% of its work is writes, so the engine that writes least and sequentially wins decisively, and it does not care that its space is 10× because it is on cheap disk. Notice that leveled LSM loses the firehose to both others — its write cost (~50) is as high as the B-tree's random write, because rewriting T×L bytes sequentially can cost as much as writing 1 byte randomly; leveled is the read-optimized LSM, not the write-optimized one. The model has no engine that wins everywhere, which is the whole point: the winner is a function of the weights.

Trap: “we benchmarked it and X won” — the benchmark that measured the wrong workload

The most expensive storage-engine mistakes come not from bad engines but from good benchmarks run on the wrong workload, and the trap has three faces. First, the synthetic benchmark that isn't your workload: a uniform-random point-read benchmark will make an LSM with Bloom filters look equal to a B-tree and hide the range-scan gap entirely, so a team that range-scans in production picks the LSM on a benchmark that never scanned — the model above shows the range column (1 versus 5 versus 50) that the point-read benchmark erased. Second, the write benchmark that outran compaction, or didn't: a short write benchmark may finish before compaction's write amplification and write stalls (Chapter 54) ever appear, so the LSM looks free; a long one on an undersized device shows the opposite — the same engine “wins” or “loses” depending on duration and provisioning, not merit. Third, “modern engines make the choice moot”: they do not — RocksDB, WiredTiger, and their peers still expose the compaction family, the size ratio, and the filter bits precisely because the RUM tension (Chapter 54) is a law, not an implementation gap, and a default tuned for someone else's workload is still the wrong point for yours. The counter-trap is equal and opposite: do not over-trust the tidy model of this chapter either — its cost vector is illustrative arithmetic with one hardware constant, and real values move with key size, value size, cache hit rate, concurrency, and the device, none of which the model captures. The honest posture is to use the model to reason about which amplification your workload spends, then measure the candidate engines on a benchmark that reproduces your real read/write/range mix, at production scale and duration, on the real device — and to distrust any benchmark whose workload you cannot map onto your own.

Say it like a systems engineer

Try it first. A teammate proposes: “Let's standardize the whole platform on one storage engine to cut operational complexity — and since our biggest service is a write-heavy event pipeline, let's make it a write-optimized LSM everywhere, including the analytics warehouse and the config store.” Cover the paragraph and answer in four or five sentences, drawing on §56.2–§56.4.

Model answer. “Standardizing on an LSM is right for the event pipeline — 80% writes means it wants the cheapest sequential write, and a size-tiered LSM beats a B-tree there by roughly 2.5× in our model. But applying it everywhere spends the wrong amplification for the other two stores. The analytics warehouse is range-scan heavy, and an LSM merges many runs where a B-tree scans one clustered run — a gap Bloom filters can't close, so we'd pay several times the scan cost. The config store is read-heavy and small; a leveled LSM would be fine, but it's a near tie with a B-tree, so we'd gain little and inherit compaction tuning we don't need. The operational-simplicity goal is legitimate, but the honest version is per-store, not one-size: size-tiered LSM for the firehose, a B-tree (or a range-friendly engine) for analytics, and either for the config store — and we should confirm each on a benchmark that matches its real read/write/range mix, not the pipeline's.” Naming the per-workload amplification, the range-scan gap the filter can't close, and the measure-your-own-mix rule is the senior register.

56.4 Reading the verdicts — and where the model lies

The model earns its keep as a reasoning tool: it forces you to name your workload's weights and then makes the RUM trade arithmetic, which is worth more than any single verdict. But a competent engineer knows exactly where it is a lie by omission. It charges a single number per operation, but real cost depends on key and value size (large values make write amplification hurt more and space amplification dominate), on cache hit rate (a hot working set served from the buffer pool erases most read amplification for both engines), on concurrency (the B-tree's latch contention and the LSM's compaction threads compete differently under load), and on the device (the ~48× sequential/random ratio is one machine's; on some NVMe it is smaller, on spinning disk far larger, and the whole balance shifts with it). It also treats the LSM as two fixed points when it is really a curve: the size ratio T and the filter bits slide it continuously between the leveled and size-tiered corners (Chapters 54–55), so “LSM” is a family to tune, not a single choice. And it ignores everything above the storage engine — durability, transactions, the query layer — which can dominate the real decision. So the discipline is two-step: use the model to decide which amplification your workload spends and therefore which region of the design space to shortlist, then measure the shortlisted engines on a benchmark that reproduces your true read/write/range mix, value sizes, working-set hit rate, and concurrency, at production scale and duration, on the real device. The model narrows the field to the right corner; measurement picks the point within it. That two-step — reason with the trade, then measure the candidates — is the whole of storage-engine judgment, and it is exactly why production engines ship the choice as a knob rather than a default.

56.5 What to carry forward

The storage-engine choice has no workload-free answer, for the same reason the RUM conjecture has none: an engine is a point (the B-tree) or a curve (the LSM family) in read/write/space cost space, and “best” is defined only once the workload supplies the weights (§56.1). Reduced to a cost vector — point read, range scan, write, space — the three engines have exact personalities: all tie on point reads (the Bloom filter's gift); the B-tree owns the range scan; size-tiered LSM writes cheapest (little and sequential) while leveled writes as dearly as a B-tree's random write; and space runs from leveled's ~1.1 to size-tiered's ~10 (§56.2). Weighting those vectors by a workload's operation mix (§56.3) picks the engine: the model gives the B-tree to read- and range-heavy stores, the size-tiered LSM to the write firehose, and a near tie on the read-heavy store — three workloads, three winners, the RUM tension resolved by the weights. And the named trap is the benchmark run on the wrong workload: use the model to reason about which amplification you spend, then measure the shortlist on your real mix, scale, and device, because the model omits key/value size, cache hit rate, concurrency, and the device ratio, and treats the tunable LSM as two fixed points (§56.4).

This closes the storage engines. Across six chapters the part built two families from the device up — the B-tree's sorted, in-place pages (Chapters 51–52) and the LSM-tree's memtable, immutable runs, compaction, and Bloom filters (Chapters 53–55) — and this chapter turned them into a decision procedure grounded in one honest law: you cannot minimize read, write, and space at once, so you choose which to spend. That is the deliverable of the whole part: not a favorite engine, but the judgment to pick and tune one for a workload. Yet every engine here has quietly assumed something we have not paid for. The B-tree updates a page in place and the LSM-tree flushes a memtable and compacts runs — and all of it lives, until the moment it reaches the disk, in the volatile buffer pool and memtable of Chapters 48 and 53. What happens when the machine loses power mid-write? A B-tree page half-updated, a memtable of acknowledged writes never flushed, a compaction interrupted — every structure the part built can be left inconsistent or simply gone by a crash we have so far pretended never happens. The next part confronts it head-on: durability and recovery — the write-ahead log, checkpoints, and the recovery algorithm that let a database promise a write survives a crash and prove it keeps that promise. The engines store the data; durability is how they keep it.
