Chapter 59 — Checkpoints: Bounding Recovery Time

Before you start: Chapter 57 (replaying the whole log from the beginning — the recovery this chapter must bound), Chapter 58 (redo, undo, the page LSN, and the dirty page — the state a checkpoint summarizes), Chapter 48 (the buffer pool and background flushing — the process that makes checkpoints possible), Chapter 41 (fsync and durability — what a checkpoint must and must not force).  ·  Chapters 57–58 gave a correct recovery, but a naive one: it scans the log from record one. That is fine for a thousand records and ruinous for a log that has grown for months. The checkpoint is how a database bounds recovery time — a periodic record that lets recovery start its scan near the end of the log instead of the beginning, without ever weakening the durability contract.

Recovery in Chapters 57 and 58 worked, but paid a price hidden by the small examples: it scanned the log from the very first record. Redo had to re-examine every logged change, if only to skip it by the page LSN; analysis had to see every transaction to know who committed. For a store that has been running for months, the log holds hundreds of millions of records, the overwhelming majority describing changes that reached their data pages long ago — durable, irrelevant to recovery, and yet re-scanned on every crash. Recovery time would grow with the entire history of the database, so the longer a system had run reliably, the longer it would take to come back from a failure — an absurd and unacceptable coupling. The fix is to periodically record a summary of what recovery would otherwise have to reconstruct from scratch — which transactions are active and which pages are dirty — so that recovery can begin near the end of the log, at a known-safe point, and ignore the long durable prefix. That summary is a checkpoint. This chapter builds it: why replay-from-the-start does not scale and what recovery actually needs to know (§59.1); the simple sharp checkpoint that works but freezes the database, and why that is unacceptable (§59.2); the fuzzy checkpoint that records the active-transaction and dirty-page tables without stalling, letting recovery start at a computed RedoLSN — demonstrated on a log where a checkpoint lets redo skip the overwhelming majority of records (§59.3); and the recLSN that makes the skip correct, plus the frequency trade-off it creates (§59.4). The result bounds recovery time to the work since the last checkpoint, whatever the log's total length.

On the code in this chapter. One Python program (CPython 3.10.12 on this machine's 12th Gen Intel Core i7-12700H) that models a running store: 2,040 update records across 200 pages under a skewed workload (a small hot set takes most writes), with a background flusher that periodically writes back the oldest-dirtied page, and a fuzzy checkpoint taken near the end that snapshots the dirty-page table. It computes the RedoLSN — the smallest recLSN in that table — and counts how many log records redo must consider starting from record one (no checkpoint) versus starting from the RedoLSN (with checkpoint), reporting the fraction skipped. It also verifies that no change below the RedoLSN is still un-flushed, so the skip loses nothing. This is a deterministic, structural demonstration — the record counts and the safety guard are exact, computed properties of the constructed log, not sampled measurements — not a wall-clock timing (perf is not installed on this machine, and the quantity of interest is records-scanned, a structural count). The point is which records recovery can safely ignore, established by construction, not the elapsed time of a run.

59.1 Why replaying from the beginning does not scale

Recovery's cost, in Chapters 57 and 58, was proportional to the length of the log, because both the analysis and redo passes began at the first record. That coupling is the problem. A busy database appends log records forever; the log is a history of every change ever made, and left unchecked it grows without bound (in practice old segments are archived or recycled, but recovery still cannot assume the live log is short). Yet almost none of that history is relevant to recovery. Consider what recovery actually needs to reconstruct. For redo, it needs the changes that might not be on their data pages yet — but the buffer pool has been flushing dirty pages back all along (Chapter 48), so a change made an hour ago on a page that was written back half an hour ago is already durable; redoing it is pure waste, correctly skipped by the page LSN but only after the record has been read. For analysis, recovery needs to know which transactions were in flight at the crash — but a transaction that committed and ended millions of records ago is settled; its fate is not in doubt. So the vast prefix of the log describes changes already on disk and transactions already resolved. The only records that matter are recent ones: changes to pages still dirty in the buffer pool, and transactions still active. If the database could record, from time to time, which pages are still dirty and which transactions are still active, recovery could start from that record and ignore everything before it — turning recovery cost from “proportional to all history” into “proportional to the work since the last such record.” That record is the checkpoint, and the two tables it must contain are exactly those two lists.

59.2 The sharp checkpoint, and why it freezes the database

The most direct way to make a checkpoint is to force the database into a clean, known state and write down that it is clean. A sharp (or quiescent) checkpoint does exactly that: stop accepting new operations, wait for all in-flight transactions to finish, flush every dirty page in the buffer pool to disk, and then append a checkpoint record. After it, the on-disk data is completely up to date — no page is dirty, no transaction is in flight — so recovery from a crash need only start at that checkpoint record: there is provably nothing before it to redo (every earlier change is on its page) and no earlier transaction to undo (all finished). It is simple and correct, and it is used nowhere serious, because of what it costs to take. Quiescing the database means refusing new work until every current transaction commits or aborts — an unbounded stall, since one long-running transaction holds the whole system hostage — and flushing every dirty page means a burst of exactly the scattered random writes (Chapter 46, ~19 MB/s) that no-force existed to avoid, now concentrated into one stop-the-world event. A checkpoint frequent enough to keep recovery fast would freeze the database constantly; one rare enough to avoid the freezes would leave recovery scanning a long tail. The sharp checkpoint trades the wrong thing: it buys short recovery with long stalls during normal running, when normal running is the case that matters ten-thousand-to-one. What is needed is a checkpoint that records enough for recovery to start late without stopping the database or forcing all its dirty pages — a checkpoint taken while the system runs.

The same idea three ways: record what is dirty and active, so recovery can start late

1. In words. Periodically, without stopping, write down the transactions still active and the pages still dirty (with, for each dirty page, the oldest log record that might not be on it yet). Recovery then begins analysis at the checkpoint and begins redo at the earliest such record, ignoring the entire durable prefix before it.

2. As a picture. Figure 59.1: a long log line. Far to the left, a vast greyed-out prefix marked “already on disk — skip.” A checkpoint marker sits near the right; a RedoLSN pointer, computed from the dirty-page table, sits a little to its left. Recovery reads only the short segment from the RedoLSN to the end.

3. As numbers. A running store logs 2,040 update records; a fuzzy checkpoint near the end captures a dirty set of 16 pages and yields a RedoLSN of 1,964. Redo without a checkpoint would scan all 2,040 records; with the checkpoint it scans 77 — skipping 96.2% of the log — and the safety guard confirms 0 un-flushed changes hide below the RedoLSN (§59.3).

59.3 The fuzzy checkpoint: record the tables, keep running

A fuzzy checkpoint drops the sharp checkpoint's two expensive demands — quiescing and flushing everything — and keeps only what recovery truly needs: a written record of the state of play. It records two tables. The active-transaction table lists every transaction currently in flight and, for each, the LSN of its most recent log record (so undo can find where to start rolling it back). The dirty-page table lists every page currently dirty in the buffer pool and, for each, its recLSN — the LSN of the oldest log record whose change might not yet be on that page, set when the page first became dirty after last being clean. Crucially, the database keeps running the whole time: transactions commit, pages are flushed, new pages are dirtied, all while (and after) the checkpoint is written. The checkpoint does not claim the disk is up to date — it is “fuzzy” precisely because dirty pages remain — it claims only these transactions were active and these pages were dirty as of here, which is exactly what recovery needs to reconstruct its starting tables without scanning from the beginning. The store models this and measures the payoff:

checkpoints: bounding recovery
  total update records in log: 2040
  pages dirty at checkpoint (DPT size): 16 of 200
  last LSN: 2041   checkpoint LSN: 2001   RedoLSN (min recLSN): 1964
  redo scans WITHOUT checkpoint (from LSN 1): 2040 records
  redo scans WITH checkpoint (from RedoLSN): 77 records
  fraction of the log skipped by the checkpoint: 96.2%
  changes below RedoLSN that are still in the DPT (must be 0): 0

The numbers tell the story. Of 200 pages, only 16 are dirty at the checkpoint — the hot working set — because the background flusher has been steadily writing the rest back and they left the dirty-page table when they went clean. The RedoLSN, the smallest recLSN over those 16 pages, is 1,964: no change logged before record 1,964 can be missing from disk, because every page it touched has since been flushed (or was never in the dirty set). So redo can begin at 1,964 and scan just 77 records to the end, instead of all 2,040 from record one — it skips 96.2% of the log. That fraction is the whole value of the checkpoint: recovery's redo work is now proportional to the un-flushed tail (77 records), not the total history (2,040), and it would stay near 77 even if the log held a billion records, because the flusher keeps the dirty set — and hence the RedoLSN — near the end. The last line is the safety check that this skip is sound: it counts changes below the RedoLSN that are still dirty (not yet on disk), and finds 0. Nothing was skipped that needed redoing — which is a property of how the RedoLSN is chosen, the subject of §59.4. This is the answer to the opener: the database periodically records the dirty-page and active-transaction tables, and recovery starts redo at the smallest recLSN, safely skipping the durable prefix.

Trap: the recLSN set to the wrong moment — a RedoLSN that starts recovery too late

The checkpoint's safety rests entirely on the recLSN meaning the right thing, and the classic corruption bug is setting it to the wrong LSN. The recLSN of a dirty page must be the LSN of the first change made to it since it was last clean — the oldest change that might not be on disk. Set it there, and the RedoLSN (the minimum recLSN) sits at or before every un-flushed change, so redo starting at the RedoLSN reconstructs them all. The tempting mistake is to record instead the page's current page LSN — the LSN of its most recent change — reasoning that “that's where the page is up to.” This is silently catastrophic: it moves the recLSN forward past earlier un-flushed changes on the same page, so the RedoLSN jumps ahead of changes that are not yet on disk, and redo skips them. The result is lost committed writes with no error at recovery time — the store comes back “successfully,” missing data, the worst possible failure because it is invisible. (The safety line in the run — “changes below the RedoLSN still dirty: 0” — is exactly the assertion that this bug is absent.) The mirror mistake is harmless but wasteful: set the recLSN too early (or never advance it as pages flush) and the RedoLSN drifts toward record one, so recovery redoes durable changes — slow, not wrong. The discipline: recLSN is the oldest-dirty LSN, updated when a clean page is first dirtied and cleared when the page is flushed; the RedoLSN is their minimum; and a checkpoint that gets this backward trades a fast recovery for a silent one.

Say it like a systems engineer

Try it first. A teammate proposes: “Recovery is taking longer every month as the log grows. Let's just checkpoint by pausing writes, flushing the whole buffer pool, and truncating the log — do it every few minutes and recovery is always fast.” Cover the paragraph and answer in four or five sentences, drawing on §59.2–§59.4.

Model answer. “You've correctly diagnosed the problem — recovery scales with the log because it starts from the beginning — but that's a sharp checkpoint, and pausing writes plus flushing the whole pool every few minutes is a stop-the-world stall that punishes the common case, normal running, to speed up the rare one. One long transaction can stretch the pause unboundedly, and the full flush is exactly the burst of random writes no-force exists to avoid. The right tool is a fuzzy checkpoint: without pausing, record the active-transaction table and the dirty-page table (each dirty page's oldest-un-flushed LSN, its recLSN); recovery then starts redo at the minimum recLSN and skips the durable prefix — in our model, 96% of the log — while the database never stops. And don't truncate the log at the checkpoint: pages are still dirty (that's why it's fuzzy), so records before the checkpoint may still be needed; you can only recycle log space behind the RedoLSN. So: keep the frequency, drop the pause and the full flush, and record the two tables instead.” Naming fuzzy versus sharp, the recLSN, and the RedoLSN is the senior register.

59.4 recLSN, the RedoLSN, and the frequency trade-off

The correctness of the whole scheme reduces to one invariant about the recLSN. For every page, the recLSN is the oldest LSN whose change may not have reached that page; equivalently, every change with an LSN below a page's recLSN is already on that page. Take the minimum recLSN across all dirty pages — the RedoLSN — and you have an LSN below which no change on any page can be un-flushed: any earlier change either touched a page not currently dirty (so it was flushed and is durable) or touched a dirty page but predates that page's recLSN (so, by the invariant, it too is on the page). That is precisely why redo may start at the RedoLSN and lose nothing — the guarantee the run's “0 un-flushed changes below RedoLSN” check confirms. A dirty page's recLSN is set when it transitions clean-to-dirty and is cleared (the page leaves the dirty-page table) when the flusher writes it back; the checkpoint simply snapshots this live table, and analysis after a crash rebuilds it by replaying from the checkpoint forward. Notice what the checkpoint did not require: it did not flush any page, so the recLSN and RedoLSN reflect ongoing lazy flushing rather than a forced burst — the fuzzy checkpoint's whole advantage.

This buys a tunable knob rather than a free lunch. Checkpoint frequency trades steady-state overhead against recovery time. Checkpoint often and the RedoLSN stays close to the log's end, so recovery is short — but each checkpoint costs a little work and log space, and, more importantly, keeping the RedoLSN near the end requires the flusher to keep the dirty set small, which means writing dirty pages back sooner (giving up some of no-force's batching). Checkpoint rarely, or let dirty pages linger, and normal-running overhead drops — but the dirty set grows, the RedoLSN drifts back, and recovery lengthens. Real systems expose this directly (a target recovery time, or a bound on log-to-replay, or a maximum dirty-page count) and let a background process hold the RedoLSN within the target. The essential shift is what recovery time now depends on: not the age of the database or the total length of its log, but the distance from the RedoLSN to the log's end — the amount of un-flushed, unresolved work — which the checkpoint and the flusher together keep bounded. Recovery that once grew with all of history now grows only with the recent past.

59.5 What to carry forward

Replaying the log from the beginning couples recovery time to the entire history of the database, because analysis and redo both start at record one, even though almost every record describes a change long since flushed and a transaction long since resolved (§59.1). A sharp checkpoint — quiesce, flush every dirty page, record that the disk is clean — makes recovery start late but freezes the database to take it, punishing the common case (§59.2). A fuzzy checkpoint records only the active-transaction table and the dirty-page table (each dirty page's recLSN) without pausing or flushing, letting recovery begin analysis at the checkpoint and redo at the RedoLSN, the minimum recLSN — in the model, skipping 96.2% of a 2,040-record log while a safety check confirms nothing un-flushed hid below it (§59.3). The recLSN must be the oldest-un-flushed LSN per page; setting it to the current page LSN moves the RedoLSN too far forward and silently loses data (§59.4). The named trap: a recLSN set to the wrong moment yields a fast recovery that is missing writes and reports success.

Chapters 57 through 59 have assembled every ingredient of a real recovery method, but as separate parts: the write-ahead log and redo (57), the before-image, undo, the LSN, and the page LSN (58), and now the checkpoint with its two tables and the RedoLSN (59). What remains is to fuse them into one algorithm with a precise, restartable protocol — an analysis pass that starts at the checkpoint and rebuilds the transaction and dirty-page tables to find the losers and the RedoLSN; a redo pass that starts at the RedoLSN and repeats history using the page LSN; and an undo pass that rolls back the losers while logging compensation records so that undo is itself redoable and recovery survives a crash mid-recovery. That algorithm is ARIES (Mohan et al. 1992), and the checkpoint of this chapter is exactly what anchors its first pass. Chapter 60 puts the three passes together and runs them — including a crash during undo — to show the whole method is correct even when recovery itself is interrupted.
