Chapter 60 — ARIES: Analysis, Redo, and Undo

Before you start: Chapter 57 (write-ahead logging and redo — the durable foundation), Chapter 58 (redo, undo, the LSN, and the page LSN — the two operations and the numbering ARIES fuses), Chapter 59 (the fuzzy checkpoint, the dirty-page table, and the RedoLSN — what anchors the first pass), Chapter 48 (the buffer pool and steal — why uncommitted changes can be on disk).  ·  Chapters 57–59 built every ingredient separately. This chapter assembles them into one algorithm — ARIES (Mohan et al. 1992) — whose three passes, analysis, redo, and undo, recover a steal + no-force database correctly, and remain correct even when a crash strikes during recovery itself.

(3) Predict: recovery has rolled back half of a loser's changes when the machine crashes again, mid-rollback. ARIES — Algorithm for Recovery and Isolation Exploiting Semantics — is the recovery method that turned the pieces of the last three chapters into a single, provably correct protocol, and it is the design real databases actually run. Its structure is three sequential passes over the log. Analysis reads forward from the last checkpoint, rebuilding the transaction table and the dirty-page table as of the crash, to determine two things: which transactions are losers (in flight at the crash, to be undone) and the RedoLSN (where redo must begin). Redo reads forward from the RedoLSN and repeats history: it re-applies every logged change — committed or not, winner or loser — whose LSN exceeds its page's page LSN, reconstructing the exact page contents that existed at the moment of the crash. Undo then reads backward, rolling back the losers' changes, and — the detail that makes the whole thing robust — logs a compensation log record for each undo it performs, so that undo is itself redoable and can resume rather than restart if recovery is interrupted. The governing idea is Mohan's phrase, repeat history: rather than trying to selectively redo only the winners, ARIES first faithfully reconstructs the entire pre-crash state, then cleanly undoes the losers on top of it — a factoring that makes each pass simple and, crucially, restartable. This chapter walks the three passes (§60.1); details analysis and the tables it rebuilds (§60.2); runs redo, repeating history, on a working store (§60.3); and builds undo with compensation records, then crashes during undo and re-runs recovery to prove it reaches the identical state (§60.4). By the end you have a recovery method that keeps durability and atomicity across a crash — including a crash in recovery.

On the code in this chapter. One Python program (CPython 3.10.12 on this machine's 12th Gen Intel Core i7-12700H) implementing a small ARIES-style recovery manager over a page store. Its durable inputs are the write-ahead log and the data pages that happened to reach disk under steal + no-force, each stamped with a page LSN. It runs a workload of three transactions — one committing, two losers — with pages stolen to disk carrying uncommitted values and a committed change lost from memory, then recovers in three passes: analysis (find losers and the RedoLSN), redo (repeat history via the page LSN), and undo (roll back losers, logging a compensation record per undo). It then crashes partway through the undo pass and re-runs recovery on the persisted log — now containing the compensation records written so far — and checks that the final state is identical to a clean recovery and equals the committed-only state. This is a deterministic, structural demonstration — recovery either reaches the committed-only state (and the restart reaches the same state) or it does not, and it does — not a wall-clock timing (perf is not installed on this machine, and the claim is correctness and restartability, not speed).

60.1 The three passes, and why history is repeated

ARIES recovers in three passes over the log, in a fixed order, each with one job. Analysis comes first and looks backward in effect but scans forward: starting from the most recent checkpoint (Chapter 59), it replays the log to the end, maintaining the transaction table (who was active, and their most recent LSN) and the dirty-page table (which pages were dirty, and their recLSN). At the end it knows the losers — transactions with no commit record, whose changes must be undone — and the RedoLSN, the smallest recLSN, where redo must begin. Redo comes second, scanning forward from the RedoLSN, and does something that surprises people first meeting ARIES: it re-applies every change it finds whose LSN exceeds the target page's page LSN, regardless of whether the transaction committed. It redoes losers' changes too. Undo comes third, scanning backward from the end, and rolls back exactly the losers, restoring each change's before-image. Winners are never touched by undo; losers are fully reversed.

Why redo the losers, only to undo them moments later? Because it makes recovery uniform and restartable, and the cost is negligible. This is ARIES's defining principle, repeat history: redo reconstructs the exact page state that existed at the instant of the crash — every change that had been made, committed or not — so that when undo runs, it operates on precisely the pages the running system had, and every page's page LSN is a correct, complete high-water mark. The alternative, trying to redo only winners, would require redo to know each transaction's fate before repeating its changes, would leave pages in a state that never actually existed, and would break the page-LSN accounting that makes redo idempotent (Chapter 58). By repeating all history first and undoing losers second, ARIES keeps each pass a simple, mechanical sweep — analysis counts, redo re-applies by page LSN, undo reverses by transaction — and, because both redo and undo are logged and idempotent, the whole sequence can be interrupted and restarted any number of times. Repeating history is not wasted work; it is the factoring that buys simplicity and restartability, which for a recovery algorithm are worth far more than saving a few redundant redos.

60.2 Analysis: rebuilding the tables, finding losers and the RedoLSN

Analysis turns a log and a checkpoint into the two facts redo and undo need. It begins at the last checkpoint record, seeding its transaction table and dirty-page table from the tables the checkpoint recorded (Chapter 59), then scans forward to the end of the log, updating them as it goes. For each update record it sees, it notes the transaction as active (recording that record's LSN as the transaction's most recent) and, if the record's page is not already in the dirty-page table, adds it with this record's LSN as the page's recLSN — the oldest change that might not be on that page. For each commit (or end) record, it marks the transaction committed (or removes it). When the scan reaches the end of the log — the point of the crash — the transaction table holds exactly the transactions that were in flight, and every one still marked active-and-uncommitted is a loser. The dirty-page table holds the pages that may not be fully on disk, and the minimum recLSN over it is the RedoLSN. That is all analysis produces: the loser set for undo, and the RedoLSN for redo.

Two subtleties matter. First, the checkpoint's tables are a starting point, not the truth: the database kept running after the checkpoint was written, so analysis must replay forward to bring the tables up to the crash — adding transactions that began after the checkpoint, and pages dirtied after it. Second, analysis does not touch data pages at all; it only reads the log and builds tables in memory. It is cheap — a single forward scan of the tail from the checkpoint — and it commits recovery to nothing, which is why it is safe to redo from scratch if recovery is interrupted before redo begins. With the loser set and the RedoLSN in hand, recovery knows precisely what redo must reconstruct and what undo must reverse. In the working store, analysis finds the two uncommitted transactions as losers and computes the RedoLSN from the dirty-page table; the next section runs redo from there.

(Answer to the §60.1 quick check: the passes are analysis (find losers and the RedoLSN), redo (repeat history — re-apply every change past its page LSN), and undo (roll back losers). Redo re-applies losers' changes because “repeat history” reconstructs the exact page state at the crash, giving undo a consistent basis and every page a correct page LSN; skipping losers would demand redo know each transaction's fate, would produce page states that never existed, and would break the page-LSN accounting that makes redo idempotent.)

The same idea three ways: analysis finds the work, redo repeats history, undo reverses losers

60.3 Redo: repeating history from the RedoLSN

Redo reconstructs the crash-time state. Scanning forward from the RedoLSN, for each update (and, later, each compensation) record, it loads the target page and compares the record's LSN to the page's page LSN: if the record is newer, it applies the change and advances the page LSN; if not, the change is already on the page and it skips (Chapter 58's idempotence). It does this for winners and losers alike — repeating history. In the working store, three transactions ran: T1 wrote P1.a=10 and P2.c=20 and committed; loser T2 wrote P1.a=777; loser T3 wrote P3.e=30 and P2.d=40; then the machine crashed. Under steal + no-force, pages P1 and P3 were stolen to disk carrying the losers' uncommitted values, while P2 was never flushed, losing T1's committed c=20. Clean recovery runs all three passes:

ARIES: analysis, redo, undo
  durable at crash -- on-disk pages (STEAL): {'P1': {'a': 777}, 'P3': {'e': 30}}
  committed-only target: {('P1', 'a'): 10, ('P2', 'c'): 20}

Clean recovery (no second crash):
  ANALYSIS  losers=['T2', 'T3']  RedoLSN=1  DPT={'P1': 1, 'P2': 2, 'P3': 5}
  REDO      re-applied 2 records from RedoLSN forward
  UNDO      rolled back losers, wrote 3 CLRs
  final state: {('P1', 'a'): 10, ('P2', 'c'): 20}
  final == committed-only: True

Analysis names T2 and T3 losers (neither committed) and computes RedoLSN=1 from the dirty-page table. Redo scans from record 1 and re-applies 2: T1's P2.c=20 (committed, lost with the unflushed page) and T3's P2.d=40 (a loser's change, also lost) — the two changes that page P2 was missing. It skips the rest: P1.a and P3.e are already on their stolen pages, their page LSNs already covering those records. After redo, the pages hold exactly what they held at the crash — P1={a:777}, P2={c:20,d:40}, P3={e:30} — losers' uncommitted values included. That is repeating history: the state is faithfully the pre-crash state, not yet the correct final one. Undo then reverses the losers (next section), restoring P1.a to 10 and deleting P2.d and P3.e, landing on P1.a=10, P2.c=20 — the committed-only target, True. Redo did not decide anything about correctness; it only rebuilt the ground truth on which undo operates. The separation is the point: redo makes the pages match the crash, undo makes the transactions match their outcomes.

Trap: undo that isn't logged — the compensation record you skip is the corruption on the next crash

The seductive shortcut in undo is to treat it as private cleanup: just write each before-image back to its page and move on, without logging anything, since “we're only restoring old values.” It works perfectly until a crash lands during undo — and then it corrupts. Suppose undo has reversed three of a loser's five changes when the machine crashes. Nothing recorded that those three were reversed. On restart, analysis re-derives the same loser (it still has no commit record), redo repeats history — which re-applies the very changes undo just removed, because the log still contains the original updates and the pages were re-stolen — and undo starts over from the loser's last change, reversing all five, some for the second time. If any change is not self-inverse (an increment, a counter, a structural update to a shared page), the double-undo corrupts it, and worse, undo may run past a value that a later, already-undone action depended on. The fix is the compensation log record (CLR): as undo reverses each change, it logs the reversal — a redo-only record carrying the value it wrote and, critically, an undoNextLSN pointing to the next change still to be undone for that transaction. Now undo's progress is durable. On restart, redo replays the CLRs too (they are ordinary redo records, idempotent via the page LSN), restoring the pages to exactly where undo had reached; and undo, following the undoNextLSN chain, resumes at the next un-undone change rather than restarting — so no change is ever undone twice, and undo never needs to be undone. A CLR is never itself rolled back; that one-way property is what bounds recovery and guarantees termination. Undo that isn't logged is a rollback that passes every test until the one crash that lands inside it.

Say it like a systems engineer

Try it first. A teammate reviewing the recovery code asks: “Why are we writing more log records during recovery? We're rolling transactions back — undoing work — so logging the undos seems backwards. If we crash again we'll just recover again; why not keep undo simple and silent?” Cover the paragraph and answer in four or five sentences, drawing on §60.1 and §60.4.

Model answer. “Because recovery can itself be interrupted, and silent undo isn't restartable. If we crash halfway through rolling a transaction back with no record of how far we got, the next recovery repeats history — which re-applies the changes we just undid — and then undoes from the top, reversing some changes twice; anything not self-inverse gets corrupted. So we log a compensation record for each undo: it's a redo-only record that says ‘this reversal happened,’ and it carries an undoNextLSN pointing to the next change still to undo. On restart, redo replays those compensation records too — idempotently, by page LSN — putting the pages exactly where undo had reached, and undo resumes from the undoNextLSN instead of restarting. That's what makes recovery survive a crash inside recovery, and it's why compensation records are never themselves undone — that one-way rule guarantees we terminate. The extra records aren't backwards; they're the difference between a rollback that's restartable and one that only looks correct until it's interrupted.” Naming CLRs, undoNextLSN, and restartability is the senior register.

60.4 Undo, compensation records, and surviving a crash during recovery

Undo rolls the losers back, and it does so as a logged, restartable operation. Scanning the losers' changes in reverse LSN order — each transaction's records chained by a back-pointer so undo can walk them newest-to-oldest — undo restores each change's before-image to its page and immediately logs a compensation log record (CLR). The CLR is an ordinary redo record for the reversal: it carries the value undo wrote (the before-image) so redo can re-apply the reversal after a crash, and it carries an undoNextLSN pointing to the next change that still needs undoing for that transaction (the current change's predecessor in the chain, or nothing if this was the transaction's first change). When a transaction's undo reaches the end of its chain, undo writes an end record and the transaction is done. The two properties that matter: CLRs are redo-only — they are never themselves undone — and their undoNextLSN records exactly how far undo has progressed. Together these make undo survive its own interruption. The store proves it by crashing after a single CLR and re-running recovery on the persisted log:

Restart recovery (crash midway through undo, then re-run):
  ANALYSIS  losers=['T2', 'T3']  RedoLSN=1  DPT={'P1': 1, 'P2': 2, 'P3': 5}
  REDO      re-applied 2 records from RedoLSN forward
  UNDO      *** CRASH after 1 CLRs ***
  ANALYSIS  losers=['T2', 'T3']  RedoLSN=1  DPT={'P1': 1, 'P2': 2, 'P3': 5}
  REDO      re-applied 0 records from RedoLSN forward
  UNDO      rolled back losers, wrote 2 CLRs
  final state: {('P1', 'a'): 10, ('P2', 'c'): 20}
  final == committed-only: True
  restart reached the SAME state as clean recovery: True

Follow the restart. The first attempt runs analysis and redo, then in undo reverses one change (T3's P2.d=40) and logs one CLR — and crashes. That CLR is now durable in the log. The second attempt starts over: analysis again finds T2 and T3 as losers (still no commit), but this time redo re-applies 0 records — the pages already reflect all history including the one logged compensation, so every record is at or below its page LSN and is skipped (idempotence, exactly as designed). Undo then resumes: because the persisted CLR's undoNextLSN points past the already-reversed P2.d to the next un-undone change, undo does not reverse P2.d again — it continues with T3's P3.e and T2's P1.a, writing 2 more CLRs — 3 in total across both attempts, one per change, never a repeat. The final state is P1.a=10, P2.c=20, identical to the clean recovery and equal to the committed-only target — True on both counts. This is the answer to the opener: recovery avoids re-undoing by logging a compensation record with an undoNextLSN as it reverses each change, so a restart replays those records (redo) and resumes the rollback from where it left off (undo). A crash during recovery is just another crash; ARIES treats recovery as ordinary, logged, idempotent work, which is why it terminates and why it is correct no matter how many times it is interrupted.

60.5 What to carry forward

ARIES recovers in three passes. Analysis scans forward from the last checkpoint, rebuilding the transaction and dirty-page tables to find the losers and the RedoLSN (§60.2). Redo scans forward from the RedoLSN and repeats history — re-applying every change past its page LSN, winners and losers alike — to reconstruct the exact crash-time page state, which gives undo a consistent basis and keeps every page LSN a correct high-water mark (§60.1, §60.3). Undo scans backward, rolling back the losers, and logs a compensation log record per reversal, carrying the restored value (so redo can replay the reversal) and an undoNextLSN (so undo can resume, not restart) — which is what lets recovery survive a crash during recovery, demonstrated by a restart that reached the identical committed-only state, undoing each change exactly once (§60.4). CLRs are redo-only and never undone, a one-way property that guarantees termination. The named trap: undo that isn't logged corrupts on the next crash by re-undoing changes it already reversed.

ARIES completes the durability-and-recovery story this part set out to tell: a steal + no-force database, tuned for buffer-pool and commit performance, can nonetheless recover exactly — every committed write preserved, every uncommitted one erased — across any crash, including one that interrupts recovery itself. The load-bearing ideas were built in sequence: write-ahead logging and the durability contract (Chapter 57), the before-image, undo, the LSN, and the page LSN (Chapter 58), the checkpoint and the RedoLSN (Chapter 59), and now the three passes with compensation records that fuse them (Chapter 60). What remains of the part sharpens the edges — the failures beyond a clean crash (a lost or corrupted disk, not just lost memory, which brings media recovery from an archived log and backups), and the choices in what to log (physical byte-images versus logical operations versus the physiological middle ground ARIES assumes). But the core is now in place, and it points forward past durability entirely. ARIES's full name — Algorithm for Recovery and Isolation — hints at the next part: recovery was only ever half of what a transaction promises. The other half is isolation — running many transactions concurrently without their letting each other see half-finished work — the A, C, and I of ACID that the coming part on transactions and concurrency control takes up. Durability is how a database keeps its word to one writer across a crash; isolation is how it keeps its word to many writers at once.
