Chapter 61 — Physiological Logging and the Log Manager

Before you start: Chapter 58 (redo, undo, the LSN, and the page LSN — the numbering that makes redo idempotent), Chapter 60 (ARIES, repeat history, and compensation records — recovery replays log records, so each must be safe to replay), Chapter 57 (write-ahead logging and the durability contract — force the log before the commit returns), Chapter 41 (fsync and durability — the syscall a commit ultimately waits on).  ·  The last four chapters assumed a log existed and asked how to recover from it. This chapter asks the two questions we deferred: what should each log record actually contain — a byte-image, an operation, or a middle ground — and how does the log get to disk cheaply, since every commit waits on it. The answers, physiological logging and the group-committing log manager, are why ARIES's records are small and why a busy database can commit thousands of transactions a second without one fsync per commit.

Before you read on

From memory: (1) Chapter 58 — what does redo compare to decide whether to re-apply a record, and what property of redo does that comparison buy? (2) Chapter 60 — during recovery, how many times might a single redo record be applied, and why can that number be more than one? (3) Predict: suppose a redo record says only “add 5 to the counter on page P” and, after a crash, recovery replays it — but that add had already reached the page before the crash. A recovery algorithm is only as good as the records it replays, and the last four chapters quietly assumed those records were exactly what redo and undo needed: idempotent, self-contained, and cheap to produce. This chapter earns that assumption. There are three ways to describe a change in a log record. Physical logging records the affected bytes — the before-image and after-image of the page (or the changed region) — so redo means “write these bytes” and undo means “write those bytes back.” Logical logging records the operation — “insert row (5, ‘Ann’) into table T,” “add 5 to the counter” — so redo means “do the operation again” and undo means “do its inverse.” Physiological logging, the term Härder and Reuter coined in 1983 and the scheme ARIES assumes, is the middle ground: physical across pages, logical within a page — each record names one page and describes the change as an operation on that page's contents (“insert this record into slot 3”), gated by the page LSN. This chapter contrasts the three (§61.1); shows why pure physical logging is idempotent but bulky and pure logical logging is compact but not safely replayable (§61.2); builds the physiological compromise — page-oriented redo with logical undo — and demonstrates its idempotence on a running store (§61.3); and then turns to the log manager that writes these records: the log buffer, force-log-at-commit, and the group commit that amortizes the fsync every commit waits on (§61.4). By the end you know both what to put in a log record and how to get it durable without paying a disk sync per transaction.

On the code in this chapter. One Python program (CPython 3.10.12 on this machine's 12th Gen Intel Core i7-12700H) in two parts. The first part is a structural demonstration of idempotence: it applies a physical redo record, a logical redo record, and a physiological redo record twice each — the double-apply that a crash during redo forces (Chapter 60) — and reports which survive unchanged. Physical and physiological survive; the logical increment double-counts. The second part is a small wall-clock measurement of the log manager: it writes sixty-four commit records to a real file, once with an fsync per commit and once with an fsync per group of eight, and reports both the fsync count (deterministic and structural) and the elapsed time (a genuine timing via time.perf_counter, which varies run to run and reflects this machine's filesystem, not a universal constant). The idempotence results are structural facts; the timing is illustrative of a real effect, reported as measured here.

61.1 What a log record records: physical, logical, physiological

Every log record answers one question — what changed? — and there are three granularities of answer, differing in how much they lean on the page's current contents. Physical logging is the most literal: the record carries the bytes. A physical record says “page P, offset 120, before = 0x00A4, after = 0x00C8” (or, coarser, the whole before- and after-image of the page). Redo overwrites with the after-image; undo overwrites with the before-image. It depends on nothing but the page identity and the recorded bytes. Logical logging is the most abstract: the record carries the operation and its arguments — “INSERT INTO accounts VALUES (5, 100),” “increment counter c by 5” — and redo re-executes the operation while undo executes its logical inverse (“delete row 5,” “decrement by 5”). It says nothing about bytes or pages; it trusts the operation to reproduce the effect against whatever state it finds.

Physiological logging sits between them, and the name (Härder & Reuter 1983) is the definition: physical to a page, logical within it. Each record names exactly one page — that is the “physical” half, and it means a record's replay is confined to a single page whose page LSN can gate it — but describes the change as a small operation on that page's structure rather than as raw bytes: “insert this record into the page's slot array,” “delete the slot at index 3,” “set field c to 20.” That is the “logical” half, and it is what keeps the records small: the page's slot directory reshuffles itself around the insert, and the record does not have to spell out every byte that moved. The page LSN (Chapter 58) makes this middle ground safe: because a physiological record touches one page and that page carries the LSN of the last change applied to it, redo can compare and skip, exactly as it does for a physical record. Physiological logging thus keeps the idempotence of physical logging (one page, page-LSN gated) while approaching the compactness of logical logging (an operation, not a byte dump). The next section shows why neither extreme will do.

61.2 Physical and logical logging: the idempotence/compactness trade

The two extremes trade the same two properties against each other: idempotence (can a record be replayed any number of times and leave the same result?) and compactness (how many bytes does a change cost in the log?). Physical logging wins idempotence and loses compactness. A physical redo record is idempotent almost by definition: writing the after-image once or five times leaves identical bytes, so a crash during redo that replays the record is harmless even without the page LSN — the record is self-inverse against itself. But it is bulky and brittle. A one-field change can drag a full-page image into the log; worse, a physical record is tied to an exact byte layout, so if a page is reorganized (a B-tree split shuffles slots, Chapter 51), a byte-offset record from before the reorganization describes bytes that no longer mean what they meant. Physical logging over-specifies, and the log pays for it in volume and in fragility to structural change.

Logical logging wins compactness and loses idempotence — and losing idempotence is fatal for a redo record. A logical record is tiny: “increment c by 5” is a few bytes regardless of page size. But its effect depends on the current state, so replaying it is not safe. This is the opener made concrete, and the program shows it: apply the redo record “add 5 to b” once and b becomes 5; a crash during redo forces the replay (Chapter 60 — a redo record may be applied more than once), and applying it again makes b = 10. The counter is double-counted; the record had no way to know it had already taken effect.

== Idempotence: apply each redo record TWICE (a crash-during-redo replays it) ==
  physical   after double-apply: {'a': 1, 'b': 2}  idempotent: True
  logical    once: {'b': 5}  twice: {'b': 10}  idempotent: False
  physiolog. once: {'b': 5} lsn 20  twice: {'b': 5} lsn 20  idempotent: True
  record size (illustrative): physical full-page ~64 B vs physiological ~19 B

Read the three lines. The physical record carries the after-image {a:1, b:2}; applied twice, the page still holds {a:1, b:2} — idempotent: True, but it shipped the whole image to get there. The logical record carries only the operation “add 5”; applied twice it reaches {b:10} when the truth is {b:5} — idempotent: False, compact but unreplayable. The physiological record (next section) carries the operation and is gated by the page LSN: applied twice, the second apply sees the record's LSN is no longer greater than the page LSN and skips, so the page stays {b:5} — idempotent: True — while the record is a fraction of the physical one (about 19 bytes versus 64 in this illustrative encoding). Neither extreme is acceptable for a redo log: physical is safe but expensive, logical is cheap but unsafe. The resolution is not to pick one but to combine their good halves.

The same idea three ways: physical, logical, physiological

1. In words. Physical logs the bytes (idempotent, bulky, brittle to layout). Logical logs the operation (compact, but not idempotent — depends on current state). Physiological logs an operation on one named page (compact and idempotent, because the page LSN gates the single page it touches). ARIES uses physiological redo; undo can be logical within the page.

2. As a picture. Figure 61.1: a change to one field, drawn three ways. Physical: a wide box holding the whole page's before- and after-bytes. Logical: a tiny box, “+5,” floating free of any page. Physiological: a small box, “set b=5,” anchored to page P with its LSN stamped on the corner — small like the logical one, but tied to a page and its LSN like the physical one.

3. As numbers. Double-applying each redo record (the crash-during-redo case): physical stays {a:1,b:2} — idempotent; logical goes {b:5} then {b:10} — not idempotent; physiological stays {b:5} because the page LSN (20) is no longer below the record's LSN on the second try — idempotent. Record size, illustratively: ~64 B physical versus ~19 B physiological (§61.2–§61.3).

61.3 Physiological logging: page-oriented redo, logical undo

Physiological logging takes the compact half of logical and the safe half of physical, and the page LSN is the hinge. A physiological redo record names one page and carries an operation on that page's structure; because it touches exactly one page, that page's page LSN can gate it, and redo applies the record only when its LSN exceeds the page LSN — the same idempotence machinery Chapter 58 built and Chapter 60 relied on. In the program, the physiological record “insert b=5” is applied against a page whose page LSN is 10, with the record at LSN 20: the first apply sees 20 > 10, performs the insert, and advances the page LSN to 20; the replayed second apply sees 20 is not > 20 and skips. The record stayed small — it described an operation, not a page image — yet it replayed safely, because the page LSN, not the record's internal form, is what makes redo idempotent. This is the crucial realization: idempotence does not require physical records; it requires a per-page high-water mark, and a physiological record supplies a change small enough to be cheap while staying bound to a single page so that mark applies.

ARIES pairs page-oriented redo with logical undo, and the asymmetry is deliberate. Redo is page-oriented — each redo record is confined to one page and gated by its LSN — because redo must be idempotent and restartable, and confinement to a page is what lets the page LSN do that job. Undo can be logical — an operation whose effect may span more than one page as the structure has since changed — because undo runs against the live, reconstructed structure (repeat history has already rebuilt it, Chapter 60) and, being logged through compensation records, is itself made restartable by the undoNextLSN chain rather than by a per-page mark. The classic case is a B-tree insert (Chapter 51): the physical slot where a key landed may have moved after a later split, so undoing the insert “delete key 5” is a logical operation that re-locates the key in the tree as it is now, not a physical rewrite of the page as it was. Physiological logging is precisely what makes this split of duties possible: redo is physical-enough (one page, LSN-gated) to be idempotent, and undo is logical-enough to survive the structure changing underneath it. That combination is why real engines log physiologically, and it is the form every record in Chapters 57–60 tacitly had.

Trap: logical redo without a page-LSN gate — the double-applied increment that corrupts on the next crash

The tempting economy is to log redo logically to shrink the log: instead of “page P, set counter to 42,” log the operation “increment the counter by 5” — a handful of bytes, no page image, no offsets. It replays perfectly in testing, because testing usually replays each record once. Then a crash lands during redo (Chapter 60: repeat history can be interrupted and restarted), recovery re-runs, and the increment that had already reached the page is applied a second time. The counter is now 5 too high, silently — no error, no crash, just a wrong number that every later transaction reads and propagates. The same corruption hits any non-self-inverse logical redo: an append, a running total, a free-space counter on a page header. The record had no way to detect that its effect was already present, because a bare logical operation carries no notion of “already applied.” The fix is the physiological discipline: bind every redo record to a single page and gate it with that page's page LSN, so replay compares the record's LSN to the page's and skips when the change is already there. You may still describe the change as an operation (that is what keeps the record small) — but it must be an operation on one page, under the LSN gate. Logical form is fine for undo, which is made restartable by compensation records; it is poison for redo, which is made restartable only by the page LSN. Logical redo without a page-LSN gate is a log that shrinks beautifully and corrupts on the first crash that interrupts recovery.

Say it like a systems engineer

Try it first. A teammate proposes simplifying the log: “Let's just log full page images for every change — before and after. It's dead simple, it's always idempotent, and we never have to reason about slot operations or logical undo. Storage is cheap. Why are we bothering with this physiological scheme at all?” Cover the paragraph and answer in four or five sentences, drawing on §61.2 and §61.3.

Model answer. “Full page images are idempotent, you're right, and if the log were free I'd agree. But every change now drags a whole page into the log even when one field moved, so the log volume — and the bandwidth of the fsync on the commit path — balloons by the page-to-field ratio, which on a 8 KB page and a small update is enormous. It's also brittle: a physical byte-image is tied to an exact layout, so a later page reorganization makes an old image describe bytes that no longer mean the same thing. Physiological logging keeps the good half of your idea — each redo record still touches one page and is gated by that page's LSN, so it's just as idempotent — while logging the operation on the page rather than the whole image, which is a fraction of the size and survives reorganization. Undo can then be logical, relocating a key in the structure as it is now. We're not avoiding simplicity; we're avoiding paying page-sized log writes and byte-layout fragility for field-sized changes.” Naming log volume, the commit-path fsync, and the physical/physiological distinction is the senior register.

61.4 The log manager: the log buffer, force-log-at-commit, and group commit

Knowing what to log leaves the second question: how does the log get to disk, given that the durability contract (Chapter 57) makes every commit wait for its records to be truly on stable storage? The log manager owns this path. Records are first appended to an in-memory log buffer — a sequential region that grows as transactions run — and periodically flushed to the log file. The rule that makes recovery correct is force-log-at-commit (write-ahead logging's commit half): before a transaction's commit is acknowledged to the client, all of its log records up to and including the commit record must be forced to durable storage with an fsync (Chapter 41). That single fsync is the price of durability, and on real storage it is expensive — a round-trip to a device that must guarantee the bytes survive power loss. Paid once per commit, it caps throughput at roughly one commit per sync latency, which for a spinning disk was milliseconds and even for flash is far slower than the rate at which transactions can otherwise complete.

Group commit breaks that cap. The observation is that an fsync flushes everything in the log up to a point, so many transactions' commit records can ride a single sync. Rather than each committer issuing its own fsync, the log manager lets commit records accumulate briefly and forces them together: one fsync makes a whole group of commits durable at once. Each transaction still blocks until the sync that covers its commit record completes — durability is not weakened, every acknowledged commit is genuinely on disk — but the cost of the sync is shared. The program measures it: sixty-four commits with one fsync each versus one fsync per group of eight.

== Group commit: many transactions, force-log-at-commit, one fsync per group ==
  64 commits, one fsync each   : fsyncs=64  wall=0.1595 s
  64 commits, groups of 8       : fsyncs=8  wall=0.0215 s
  fsyncs reduced 8x; both durable (every commit is on disk before it returns)

The structural fact is the fsync count: 64 versus 8, an eight-fold reduction, exactly the group size — and it is deterministic, the same on any machine. The wall-clock figures (0.1595 s versus 0.0215 s on this machine's filesystem, roughly seven-fold here) are a genuine measurement of the effect but not a universal constant: the ratio tracks how dominant the sync is relative to the rest of the commit, which depends on the device. What does not change is the shape: group commit trades a small amount of latency (a committer may wait a moment for its group to fill) for a large gain in throughput (the expensive sync is amortized over the group), without ever weakening durability, because every acknowledged commit still waited for a real sync that covered its record. This is why a production log manager batches: the durability contract demands a force before every commit returns, and group commit is how to honor it without paying a separate disk sync per transaction. The log buffer, force-log-at-commit, and group commit are the three moving parts of the path that Chapters 57–60 assumed but never opened.

61.5 What to carry forward

A log record answers “what changed?” at one of three granularities. Physical logging records the bytes: idempotent by construction, but bulky and brittle to page layout (§61.2). Logical logging records the operation: compact, but not idempotent — replaying “add 5” double-counts, fatal for a redo record (§61.2). Physiological logging (Härder & Reuter 1983), the scheme ARIES assumes, is the middle ground — physical to a page, logical within it — so each redo record touches one page, is gated by that page's page LSN (hence idempotent), yet describes an operation rather than a page image (hence compact) (§61.1, §61.3). ARIES pairs page-oriented redo (LSN-gated, restartable per page) with logical undo (restartable via compensation records, able to relocate a change in a structure that has since moved) (§61.3). The log manager writes these records through a log buffer, honors force-log-at-commit, and uses group commit to amortize the per-commit fsync over a batch — an eight-fold fsync reduction in the run, with durability intact (§61.4). The named trap: logical redo without a page-LSN gate corrupts on the first crash that interrupts recovery, because the double-applied operation cannot detect that it already took effect.

With this chapter the shape of a real write-ahead log is complete: physiological records, small and idempotent, written by a batching log manager that keeps the commit path fast without breaking the durability contract. Only one gap remains in the durability-and-recovery story, and it is the failure ARIES itself does not cover. Every algorithm so far assumed the disk survived the crash — that recovery could read the data pages and the log back off stable storage. The next chapter removes that assumption: what happens when the disk itself is lost or corrupted, so there are no pages and no log to recover from? That is media recovery, and it needs two things this part has not yet named — a backup and an archived log — to roll a database forward from a copy rather than repair it in place. Chapter 62 builds it, and in doing so closes Part X, before Part XI turns from keeping a database's word across failures to keeping it across concurrency — the isolation half of the transaction that recovery alone never provided.
