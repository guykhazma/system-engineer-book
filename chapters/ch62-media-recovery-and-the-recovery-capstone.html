Chapter 62 — Media Recovery and the Recovery Capstone

Before you start: Chapter 57 (write-ahead logging and the durability contract — force the log, then the pages may follow), Chapter 59 (checkpoints and the RedoLSN — bounding crash recovery), Chapter 60 (ARIES — recovery that reads the surviving disk and log), Chapter 61 (physiological records and the log manager — the log this chapter archives).  ·  Every recovery method in this part assumed one thing: the disk survived. Analysis, redo, and undo all read pages and log records back off stable storage. This chapter removes that assumption. When the disk itself is lost or corrupted — a failed drive, a bad controller, a fat-fingered rm — there are no pages and no log to recover from, and ARIES has nothing to work with. Media recovery is how a database survives that: rebuild from a backup and an archived log kept on separate storage. This is also the capstone of Part X, where write-ahead logging, the LSN, the checkpoint, ARIES, and now media recovery assemble into one durability story.

Before you read on

From memory: (1) Chapter 60 — what two durable inputs does ARIES read to recover, and where do they live? (2) Chapter 57 — after a crash, what does the durability contract guarantee is on disk for every committed transaction? (3) Predict: the disk is physically destroyed — no data pages and no log survive on it. ARIES needs both to run, so it cannot help. What two things, if you had kept them somewhere else, would let you rebuild the database — and to what moment in time could you rebuild it? Write your guess; §62.2–§62.3 answer it.

Härder and Reuter's 1983 taxonomy names three classes of failure a database must survive, and this part has, until now, addressed only two. A transaction failure (an abort, a constraint violation) is handled by undo. A system failure (a crash: power loss, a kernel panic) loses volatile memory but not the disk, and is handled by ARIES's three passes reading the surviving pages and log. The third is media failure: the stable storage itself is lost or corrupted, so the data pages and the log that lived on it are gone. No amount of redo or undo helps, because there is nothing to read. Recovery from media failure needs inputs that were deliberately kept off the failed medium: a backup (a copy of the database as of some past moment) and an archived log (a copy of the log records, on separate storage, spanning from the backup forward). Media recovery restores the backup and then rolls the archived log forward onto it, re-applying every logged change from the backup's moment to the point you want to reach. This chapter distinguishes the failure classes and why media failure is different (§62.1); introduces backups and the archived log, the two ingredients (§62.2); performs roll-forward and point-in-time recovery, stopping at a chosen moment (§62.3); and then steps back for the capstone (§62.4), assembling the whole part — the durability contract, the LSN and page LSN, the checkpoint, ARIES, and media recovery — into one account of how a database keeps its word across every failure. By the end, Part X is complete.

On the code in this chapter. One Python program (CPython 3.10.12 on this machine's 12th Gen Intel Core i7-12700H) implementing a small page store with a redo log. It runs a workload, takes a full backup of the pages partway through, keeps archiving the log, and then simulates a media failure by discarding the on-disk pages entirely — leaving ARIES nothing to recover from. It then performs media recovery two ways: restore the backup and roll the archived log forward to the end (reaching the exact pre-failure state), and restore the backup and roll forward only to a chosen point in time (reaching an earlier state that excludes some later writes). It also shows what a backup without a log can reach — only the backup instant, losing everything after. This is a deterministic, structural demonstration: each recovery either reaches the expected state or it does not, and it does. There is no wall-clock claim here; the guarantee is state equality, not speed.

62.1 Beyond a crash: when the disk itself is lost

The recovery this part built is powerless against a lost disk, and it is worth being precise about why. ARIES's inputs are the data pages and the log, both read back from stable storage after the failure. A crash — a system failure — destroys only the volatile buffer pool; the disk is intact, so ARIES can scan the log from the checkpoint, redo forward, and undo backward, exactly as Chapter 60 showed. A media failure destroys the stable storage itself: the drive fails, the volume is corrupted, a region of the log is unreadable. Now the pages ARIES would repair are gone and the log it would replay is gone with them. Running ARIES accomplishes nothing — there is no page to compare a page LSN against, no record to redo. The program makes the point starkly by discarding the on-disk pages and observing that recovery has an empty store to work from:

Backup taken at lsn 2 -> {'P1': {'bal': 100}, 'P2': {'bal': 200}}
Live disk before failure   : {'P1': {'bal': 999}, 'P2': {'bal': 1}}  (latest lsn 6 )
After media failure         : {}   <- ARIES has nothing to recover from

The database had run to LSN 6 with live pages P1={bal:999}, P2={bal:1}. The media failure leaves {} — no pages at all. This is qualitatively different from a crash, which would have left those pages (or older versions of them) on disk for ARIES to reconcile with the log. The lesson is that durability against a crash and durability against media loss are different guarantees requiring different mechanisms. The write-ahead log on the same disk buys crash durability, because a crash preserves the disk; it buys nothing against the disk's own loss, because the log dies with it. Surviving media failure demands copies kept elsewhere — which is what the rest of the chapter builds. The failure taxonomy is not academic: it tells you exactly which mechanism defends against which failure, and it explains why a system with flawless ARIES recovery can still lose everything to a single dead drive that took the only copy of the log with it.

62.2 Backups and the archived log: the two ingredients

Media recovery has exactly two ingredients, and each answers a different half of the problem. A backup is a copy of the database — the data pages — as of some past moment, taken to separate storage. It gives recovery a starting point that survived the failed medium: a known-good state as of the backup's LSN. But a backup alone is stale — the database kept running after it was taken — so restoring only the backup loses every change since. The program shows exactly that loss:

Backup only, no archived log : {'P1': {'bal': 100}, 'P2': {'bal': 200}}  -> lost every change after lsn 2

The backup was taken at LSN 2, holding P1={bal:100}, P2={bal:200}. Restoring only it rewinds the database to that instant and discards LSNs 3 through 6 — the transfers and later writes are simply gone. This is the recovery-point cost of backups: on their own, they lose all work back to the last one. The second ingredient closes the gap. An archived log is a copy of the log records, kept on separate storage, spanning continuously from the backup forward. Because the log records every change (Chapter 61 made them physiological and idempotent), replaying the archived log onto the restored backup re-creates every change that happened after the backup — rolling forward from the backup's moment toward the present. The backup supplies the base; the archived log supplies everything since. Together they can rebuild the database to any moment the archive covers. Crucially the archived log must live on different storage than the primary — an archive on the same disk that failed is no archive at all, which is the named trap of this chapter. The two ingredients are complementary: the backup bounds how much log you must replay (you start from it, not from the beginning of time), and the archived log bounds how much data you lose (you roll forward from the backup, not stop at it).

(Answer to the §62.1 quick check: the three classes are transaction failure — handled by undo; system failure, a crash — handled by ARIES's redo and undo over the surviving disk; and media failure — handled by restoring a backup and rolling the archived log forward. The write-ahead log gives perfect crash recovery because a crash preserves the disk the log lives on; it is useless against media failure because the disk's loss takes the log with it, leaving nothing to replay.)

The same idea three ways: crash recovery, media recovery, point-in-time recovery

1. In words. Crash recovery runs ARIES over the surviving disk to reach the exact pre-crash committed state. Media recovery restores a backup and rolls the archived log forward to reach that same state after the disk is lost. Point-in-time recovery is media recovery that stops early, at a chosen moment, to reach an earlier state — the tool for undoing a bad batch that a healthy disk faithfully recorded.

2. As a picture. Figure 62.1: a timeline with a “backup” marker at LSN 2 and log records 3–6 strung along it. Crash recovery: an arrow over the live disk to LSN 6. Media recovery: restore the backup box, then a roll-forward arrow replaying 3–6 to LSN 6. Point-in-time recovery: the same roll-forward arrow, but stopping at LSN 4, leaving 5–6 unreplayed.

3. As numbers. Backup at LSN 2 = {P1:100, P2:200}. Roll forward to the end: {P1:999, P2:1} — equals the pre-failure live disk, True, six records replayed. Roll forward to LSN 4 (PITR): {P1:150, P2:150} — equals the state at LSN 4, True, four records replayed, the bad writes at LSN 5–6 excluded. Backup only: {P1:100, P2:200} — everything after LSN 2 lost (§62.2–§62.3).

62.3 Roll forward and point-in-time recovery

Rolling forward is redo against a restored backup rather than a surviving disk. Recovery loads the backup pages and replays the archived log records in LSN order, applying each change; because the records are idempotent (Chapter 61), replaying them onto the backup reconstructs exactly the changes that followed it. Replayed to the end of the archive, roll-forward reaches the precise pre-failure state:

Restore backup + replay all : {'P1': {'bal': 999}, 'P2': {'bal': 1}}  (6 records replayed)
  equals pre-failure live disk: True

From the backup {P1:100, P2:200} at LSN 2, replaying all six records lands on {P1:999, P2:1} — identical to the live disk the media failure destroyed. The database is whole again, rebuilt entirely from copies that were kept elsewhere. But roll-forward has a power crash recovery does not: it can stop early. Point-in-time recovery (PITR) replays the archived log only up to a chosen moment — an LSN, or a wall-clock timestamp recorded in the log — and halts, reaching the state as of that moment rather than the end. This is the tool for a failure the disk recorded faithfully: a bad migration, an erroneous bulk update, a deletion run against the wrong table. The disk did exactly what it was told; ARIES would loyally reproduce the damage. PITR rewinds instead, rolling forward to just before the mistake:

PITR: replay backup..lsn 4  : {'P1': {'bal': 150}, 'P2': {'bal': 150}}  (4 records replayed)
  equals the state at lsn 4   : True  (bad writes at lsn 5-6 excluded)

Stopping at LSN 4 reaches {P1:150, P2:150} — the state after the legitimate transfer but before the bad writes at LSN 5–6, which are simply never replayed. This is why backups plus an archived log are more than a media-failure defense: together they let you reconstruct the database as of any moment the archive spans, which is the mechanism behind recovering from human error and corruption that a single live disk, however durable, cannot undo. The recovery-point objective — how much data you can afford to lose — is set by how far behind the archive lags; the recovery-time objective — how fast you can be back — is set by how much log must be replayed since the last backup, which is exactly why backups are taken periodically rather than once. Roll-forward and PITR are the same operation with a different stopping rule: replay the archive from the backup, either to the end or to the moment you choose.

Trap: the log on the disk you lost — an archive that dies with the medium it was meant to survive

The seductive convenience is to keep the archived log next to the database — same volume, same array, same host — because it is simplest and the log is “already there.” It passes every crash-recovery test, because a crash preserves the disk and the log with it. Then the medium itself fails — the drive dies, the array is corrupted, the host is destroyed — and the archive you were counting on for media recovery dies in the same event, because it never left the thing it was supposed to survive. Now the backup, however faithfully taken, can only be restored to its moment; every change since is unrecoverable, because the only record of those changes went down with the disk. This is not a hypothetical: it is the difference between a real disaster-recovery plan and one that works only for failures that spare the disk. The fix is a rule with no exceptions: the archived log and the backups must live on storage that fails independently of the primary — a different volume at minimum, a different machine better, a different site or provider for true disaster recovery. And because a backup you have never restored is a hypothesis, the archive and backups must be periodically test-restored: roll a backup forward through the archive on separate hardware and assert it reaches the expected state, exactly as the program does. A backup that has never been restored and an archive that shares the disk it protects are the two ways a recovery plan looks complete and fails in the one event it existed for.

Say it like a systems engineer

Try it first. A teammate pushes back on the backup schedule: “We already have ARIES and a write-ahead log — recovery is provably correct across a crash. Why are we spending money and I/O on backups and log archiving to separate storage? Isn't the log we already write enough?” Cover the paragraph and answer in four or five sentences, drawing on §62.1 and §62.2.

Model answer. “ARIES and the WAL give us perfect recovery from a crash — a power loss or panic — because a crash keeps the disk, so we can read the pages and log back and reconcile them. They give us nothing against the disk itself being lost or corrupted, because then the pages and the log are gone together; there's nothing to replay. That's a different failure class, and it needs copies kept somewhere the failure can't reach: a backup for a known-good base, and an archived log on independent storage so we can roll forward from the backup to the present. As a bonus, those two also let us do point-in-time recovery — rewinding to just before a bad migration that a healthy disk would have faithfully reproduced. The one rule is that the archive can't live on the disk it protects, and we have to test-restore it, or it's just a hypothesis.” Naming the failure classes, independent storage, and PITR is the senior register.

62.4 The recovery capstone: the whole durability story in one pass

Part X set out to answer one question: how does a database keep its promises across failure? The pieces are now all in hand, and they compose into a single chain. A transaction commits, and the durability contract (Chapter 57) forces its log records to stable storage before the commit returns — write-ahead logging, log before data, so a crash can never acknowledge work it cannot recover. Those records are physiological (Chapter 61): small operations on single pages, each stamped with an LSN, each page carrying the page LSN of its last change (Chapter 58) — the numbering that makes redo idempotent and undo precise. To keep recovery from scanning the whole log, the database writes checkpoints (Chapter 59), each recording a RedoLSN that bounds where redo must begin. When a crash strikes, ARIES (Chapter 60) runs three passes over the surviving disk: analysis rebuilds the tables and finds the losers and the RedoLSN, redo repeats history from there, and undo rolls the losers back, logging compensation records so recovery survives a crash within recovery. And when the disk itself is lost, media recovery (this chapter) restores a backup and rolls the archived log forward — to the present, or to any point in time — from copies kept where the failure could not reach.

Trace one durable value through the whole chain and the design's coherence shows. A committed balance of 150 is safe across a crash because its log record was forced before commit (contract), redo will re-apply it if its page was lost from memory (LSN gate), and it will not be re-applied if already on disk (page LSN) — recovery starting near the end because a checkpoint bounded it (RedoLSN), and reaching the exact committed state because ARIES repeats history then undoes losers. The same balance is safe across a media failure because it lives in a backup or in the archived log on independent storage, and roll-forward reconstructs it; and it can be recovered to before a later mistake because PITR stops the roll-forward at the chosen moment. Each mechanism defends a different failure, and together they cover the space Härder and Reuter mapped: transaction, system, and media failure, each with its mechanism, no gap between them. That completeness is the point of the part — not any single algorithm, but the way the durability contract, the LSN, the checkpoint, ARIES, and media recovery interlock so that a committed change survives every failure a single node can suffer. In the program, that whole story reduces to one checkable claim: a database destroyed at the media level is rebuilt, from copies alone, to the exact state it held — and, if you choose, to any earlier state it passed through.

62.5 What to carry forward

Härder and Reuter's taxonomy names three failure classes: transaction failure (undo), system failure — a crash (ARIES over the surviving disk), and media failure — the disk lost or corrupted (§62.1). Media failure defeats ARIES because its inputs, the pages and the log, die with the disk (§62.1). Media recovery rebuilds from two ingredients kept on independent storage: a backup (a known-good base as of some past LSN) and an archived log (the records since, on separate media) (§62.2). Recovery restores the backup and rolls the archived log forward — to the end, reaching the exact pre-failure state (six records replayed to {P1:999, P2:1}), or via point-in-time recovery to a chosen moment (four records replayed to {P1:150, P2:150}, excluding a later bad write) (§62.3). The capstone (§62.4) assembles the whole part: the durability contract, the LSN and page LSN, the checkpoint and RedoLSN, ARIES's three passes with compensation records, and media recovery — each defending a different failure, together covering every failure a single node can suffer. The named trap: an archived log kept on the disk it protects dies with that disk, and a backup never test-restored is only a hypothesis.

Part X is complete. A steal + no-force database — tuned for buffer-pool and commit performance — nonetheless keeps every committed write and erases every uncommitted one across a transaction abort, a crash, a crash during recovery, and even the loss of the disk itself. That is durability, the D of ACID, built end to end. But durability was only ever half of what a transaction promises. Everything in this part concerned one writer's word kept across a failure; none of it addressed what happens when many transactions run at once and can see each other's half-finished work. ARIES's own full name — Algorithm for Recovery and Isolation — pointed here all along. Chapter 63 opens Part XI, Transactions and Concurrency Control, with the other half of the transaction: the ACID properties in full, the anomalies that appear when transactions interleave without control — the dirty read, the lost update, the non-repeatable read, the phantom — and the isolation levels that trade correctness for concurrency. Durability is how a database keeps its word to one writer across a crash; isolation is how it keeps its word to many writers at once, and it is the subject of everything that follows.
